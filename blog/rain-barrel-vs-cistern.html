Rain Barrel or Cistern? Choosing the Right Storage Size

Published: October 5, 2026 · 9 min read

A rain barrel is a 50–100 gallon starter tank you can install this afternoon; a cistern is a 500 to 10,000+ gallon permanent tank you size against your daily water use. So the choice is not really a matter of taste — it is a sizing decision. Watering containers and a couple of beds? One or two barrels under a downspout is enough, for under $200. Irrigating a real garden, watering livestock, or displacing household water? A cistern is the only option that keeps up, because a single 55-gallon barrel empties in about 20 minutes of hose time.

This guide compares them on the numbers that decide it — capacity, cost per gallon, footprint, plumbing, and freeze risk — then works out what size you need. To skip ahead, the Rainwater Harvest Calculator returns a recommended tank size from your roof area, rainfall, and daily demand.

Rain Barrels vs Cisterns at a Glance

The two are not scaled versions of each other. A barrel is a portable consumer item; a cistern is infrastructure. That difference shows up in every line below.

Factor Rain Barrel Cistern
Typical capacity 50–100 gallons each 500–10,000+ gallons
Footprint 2–4 sq ft, movable by hand 20+ sq ft, fixed and often buried
Typical cost $80–250 with a diverter $500–3,500 DIY; $4,000–15,000 installed
Cost per gallon stored $1.50–4.50 $0.30–1.80
Time to install An afternoon, no permit in most areas A weekend to a contractor; permits common
Realistic lifespan 5–15 years (UV-degraded HDPE) 20–50 years for polyethylene or concrete
Distribution Gravity, from a raised barrel Usually needs a pump
Winter handling Drain and store indoors Drain, or bury below frost depth
Best for Container plants, spot watering, testing the habit Full garden irrigation, livestock, non-potable household use

Where rain barrels win

Barrels win on speed, entry price, and reversibility. You can buy one on a Saturday, set it on cinder blocks under a downspout, and be watering with it by the afternoon — no excavation, no permit, no contractor. They are also the honest way to learn whether you will really carry a watering can around. Two unused barrels cost $200 and an afternoon instead of $3,000 and a summer.

Where cisterns win

Cisterns win on arithmetic. Storage is the whole point of harvesting rain, because rain does not arrive when you need it: roughly half your annual total falls in the wetter months while garden demand peaks in the driest ones. A 55-gallon barrel banks about a quarter inch of rain off a 1,000 sq ft roof, so one minor shower fills it and the next runs off down the driveway. A 2,500-gallon cistern banks about four inches — the difference between "I have rainwater" and "I have water."

Sizing: Start With Daily Demand, Not With the Tank

Work backward from use. Decide how many gallons you need on a peak summer day, then choose storage equal to one to two months of that demand — enough to ride out a dry spell without paying to store water you never touch.

What You Are Watering Peak Daily Demand Storage to Aim For Typical Choice
Containers, herbs, a few beds 5–15 gallons 50–100 gallons 1–2 rain barrels
500 sq ft vegetable garden 50–80 gallons 1,000–1,500 gallons 4–6 IBC totes or a 1,000-gallon cistern
1,500 sq ft garden plus orchard 100–150 gallons 2,500–4,000 gallons 2,500-gallon cistern
Backyard flock (10 hens) 1–2 gallons 50–100 gallons 1 rain barrel
Beef cattle or horses (1–2 head) 25–40 gallons 1,000+ gallons 1,000–2,500-gallon cistern
Household non-potable use (family of 4) ~300 gallons 8,000–10,000 gallons Large buried cistern

The household line surprises people. U.S. indoor use averages about 80 gallons per person per day, so a family of four routing rainwater only to toilets, laundry, and the garden needs a tank most residential lots cannot hold above ground. That is why most homesteads land in the middle of the table — garden and livestock — and stay there.

Check the supply side too: a 1,000 sq ft roof in a 40-inch rainfall area sheds about 20,700 gallons a year after typical shingle losses. If your storage target exceeds what the roof can deliver in a month, the collection area is the constraint, not the tank. What Each Option Really Costs

Cost per gallon is where barrels quietly lose. Price the same capacity both ways and the gap is hard to argue with.

Storage Option Capacity Typical Cost Cost per Gallon
DIY 55-gallon barrel 55 gal $60–100 $1.10–1.80
Retail barrel with diverter 55 gal $150–250 $2.70–4.50
Used food-grade IBC tote 275 gal $75–175 $0.27–0.64
500-gallon polyethylene cistern 500 gal $500–900 $1.00–1.80
2,500-gallon polyethylene cistern 2,500 gal $1,800–3,200 $0.72–1.28
10,000-gallon installed tank 10,000 gal $6,000–12,000 $0.60–1.20

Add accessories before comparing totals. Either system needs a first-flush diverter, gutter screens, 1/16-inch mesh over every opening, and an overflow line that carries excess away from the foundation. Those parts run $100–300 regardless of tank, which is why a $70 barrel often becomes a $200 project. And if 500 gallons feels like a jump, a used 275-gallon food-grade IBC tote — $75–175, daisy-chainable to 1,000 gallons — is the cheapest capacity anywhere and the usual middle step before a permanent cistern. Verify it is genuinely food grade: totes that held detergents or industrial chemicals can leach residues into your water.

The Differences That Bite Later

Overflow is a design feature

Every tank fills. An overflow that dumps at the foundation can undermine a slab, and a cistern without one can back up into gutters and destroy fascia. Decide where the excess goes — a swale, a rain garden, or a second tank — before you set anything in place.

Mosquitoes and algae

Standing water breeds mosquitoes, so every inlet, overflow, and vent needs 1/16-inch or finer mesh. Algae is a light problem: opaque polyethylene and shaded barrels stay clear, while translucent totes grow green film within a season unless you block the light.

Water quality, use by use

Harvested rain is fine on soil-grown vegetables and not fine in a glass without treatment. The split runs by crop rather than by tank: roots and fruit are low risk, raw leafy greens are the ones to watch, and lead flashing on the roof is the one contaminant no diverter removes. Is rainwater safe for vegetable gardens? covers the crop list and the fixes that cost under $60.

Freeze and frost

Drain and store barrels before the first hard freeze — a full barrel that freezes can split along its seam. A buried cistern handles winter if it sits below frost depth; an above-ground tank in a cold climate should be drained or kept partly empty so ice has room to expand.

Regulations go by capacity

This is the one place a cistern can be harder to permit than a barrel. Colorado is the usual cautionary example: residents may collect rooftop rain in up to two barrels totaling 110 gallons without a water-rights permit, and anything larger needs approval. Most other states have no residential restriction and several offer rebates or sales-tax exemptions, but counties and HOAs still regulate placement, height, setbacks, and screening. The statewide rules and incentive programs are covered in our Rainwater Harvesting 101 guide.

A Sensible Upgrade Path

You do not have to choose once and live with it. The cheapest sequence proves the habit before you buy the infrastructure.

  1. Year one — two rain barrels. Collect 110 gallons under the closest downspouts, water containers and new transplants, and note how often you empty them. Cost: $150–400.
  2. Year two — IBC totes or a small cistern. If the barrels empty within a week of every storm, you have your answer. Add 550–1,000 gallons of stored capacity and a first-flush diverter. Cost: $300–1,500.
  3. Year three and beyond — a sized cistern. By now you know your peak demand from real use rather than a guess, so the tank gets sized right the first time. Cost: $1,800–3,500 for a 2,500-gallon installation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a rain barrel or a cistern better?

It depends on daily water use, not on which one sounds more serious. A rain barrel is better for containers, a few beds, or testing whether you will actually use harvested rain. A cistern is better once you need more than a few days of storage, because a 55-gallon barrel empties in roughly 20 minutes of hose time. Stay with barrels under about 300 gallons of total storage and step up to a cistern at 1,000 gallons or more.

How many rain barrels equal one cistern?

By volume, a 1,000-gallon cistern equals about eighteen 55-gallon barrels, and a 2,500-gallon cistern roughly forty-five. That is why barrels rarely scale: eighteen barrels need far more footprint, plumbing, and fittings than one tank, and every daisy-chained connection is another place to leak. Cost per gallon stored usually favors a single mid-size tank.

How much does a cistern cost compared with rain barrels?

A retail 55-gallon rain barrel with a diverter typically runs 150 to 250 dollars, or about 2.70 to 4.50 dollars per gallon stored. A 500-gallon polyethylene cistern costs about 500 to 900 dollars and a 2,500-gallon tank about 1,800 to 3,200 dollars, roughly 0.70 to 1.80 dollars per gallon. A used food-grade 275-gallon IBC tote at 75 to 175 dollars is the cheapest capacity anywhere.

Do I need a pump for a cistern?

Usually yes above a few hundred gallons, because a cistern sits low or partially buried and its outlet is below the garden. A 55-gallon barrel raised two feet gives usable gravity pressure for a hose and watering can. A cistern feeding drip irrigation or a distant bed needs a transfer or small pressure pump, adding roughly 150 to 600 dollars.

Can I drink water from a rain barrel or cistern?

Not without treatment. Roof runoff carries dust, pollen, bird droppings, and whatever the shingles shed, so harvested rain is fine for gardens and livestock but not potable as stored. Making it drinkable requires first-flush diversion, sediment and carbon filtration, and UV or chlorine disinfection, typically adding 500 to 1,500 dollars. If you have a well or municipal supply, keep rainwater for non-potable use.

Bottom line: buy barrels to prove the habit, then buy the tank that matches your daily use. Watering containers and a small bed? Two barrels are genuinely enough. Irrigating a garden, watering stock, or displacing household water? The arithmetic points to a cistern — and a used IBC tote is the cheapest way to find out where you land.
