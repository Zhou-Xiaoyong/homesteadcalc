How Many Pounds of Turkey Per Person?

Published: November 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Buy one pound of raw turkey per person, or 1 1/2 pounds if you want leftovers. A 20-pound bird yields about 9 pounds of cooked meat, which is 24 plates at six ounces each or 18 at eight ounces.

Those two figures, 24 and 14, are why this question has four answers on four websites. One is counting meat. The other is counting meat plus seconds plus a week of sandwiches, and both are honest as long as you know which one you are buying.

The Bird-by-Bird Table

Cooked meat runs 42 to 48 percent of dressed weight, and the percentage climbs with the bird because a bigger frame carries proportionally more breast. Every number below uses that range rather than a flat guess.

Dressed weight Cooked meat 1 lb per person 6-oz plates 8-oz plates
12 lb 5.0 lb (42%) 12 guests 13 10
16 lb 7.0 lb (44%) 16 guests 19 14
20 lb 9.0 lb (45%) 20 guests 24 18
24 lb 11.5 lb (48%) 24 guests 30 23

Read the last two columns against your crowd. A family that takes one slice of white and one of dark eats six ounces. A table where people come back for a second plate eats eight, and the same 20-pound bird drops from 24 servings to 18.

Why a Small Bird Feeds Fewer Than Its Weight Suggests

A 12-pound turkey is not half of a 24-pounder. It is a leaner frame with a thicker bone-to-meat ratio, closer to 42 percent usable meat than 48. Two 12-pound birds give you 10 pounds of cooked meat, and one 24-pound bird gives 11.5 for the same 24 pounds on the tag.

Under 12 pounds the correction matters. At 1 pound per person a 12-pounder feeds 12, and 2 of them will go home hungry if anyone takes seconds. Bump small birds to 1 1/4 pounds per person and the arithmetic holds.

The frame is not waste. A 20-pound carcass, skin, and drippings give 3 to 4 quarts of stock, and that stock is the difference between gravy from a jar and gravy from the bird. The gravy arithmetic for a crowd starts from what the pan gives you.

What the Standard Charts Are Counting

The 1 1/2-pounds-per-person figure that shows up on Butterball and in most grocery flyers is not padded. It buys an eight-ounce plate, a second pass for two or three people, and a leftover pile big enough to be worth wrapping.

Run it backward. Twenty pounds at 1.5 pounds per person is 13 guests. Charts that print 14 to 16 for the same bird are working at 1.25 pounds, which assumes lighter portions and a smaller leftover pile. Nothing is inconsistent. They assume you want the leftovers. If you do not, 1 pound per person is the number and 20 guests fit around a 20-pound bird.

Where charts genuinely mislead is the small end. A 10-pound turkey is sold as serving 10, and at 42 percent it gives 4.2 pounds of meat, which is 11 six-ounce plates only if nobody takes dark meat twice.

Sizing When Leftovers Are the Point

Add half a pound per person to whatever the plain table gave you. For 12 guests that is 18 pounds, which yields about 8 pounds of cooked meat against 5 pounds eaten, so roughly 3 pounds go in the fridge.

Three pounds is four days of sandwiches for two people, and cooked turkey keeps 3 to 4 days refrigerated. Past that it needs to be frozen, where it holds 2 to 3 months before the texture goes stringy.

There is a limit to buying for leftovers. A 24-pound bird is 11.5 pounds of meat, and unless you are feeding 16 people first, half of it becomes a container you find in February. Two 14-pound birds give similar meat with a fresher second bird.

The Bird Your Oven, Pan, and Fridge Will Take

Pan. A 20-pound bird needs a 17-by-11 1/2-inch roasting pan with a rack. Measure the pan against the oven rack before the store, not after.

Oven. Most 30-inch ranges clear 20 to 22 pounds with 2 inches of headroom above the breast. A 24-pounder needs the rack dropped to its lowest position, and the top of the bird still browns first.

Fridge. A 20-pound bird is a block about 15 by 11 by 9 inches, and most shelves clear 10 to 12 inches. If it does not fit, the plan is a cooler with ice, not a warmer garage. The freezer and shelf measurements cover the space question in detail.

Thaw. USDA allows 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds in the refrigerator. That is 3 days for a 12-pounder, 5 days for a 20, and 6 for a 24. Twelve days out from the holiday, a 20-pound frozen bird goes into the fridge on the 21st.

Miss that window and the fallback is cold water: 30 minutes per pound, bag sealed, water changed every half hour. A 20-pounder takes 10 hours that way, which is an all-night job, not a morning one.

The home-raised line is the one people get wrong. A broad-breasted tom converts about 2.6 pounds of feed into a pound of live weight, so a 20-pound bird eats roughly 52 pounds of feed. At $22 to $24 for feed, $10 for the poult, and a few dollars of bedding, that is $39 for a 15-pound dressed bird. Cheaper than heritage retail. Three times the frozen aisle.

Dressing percentage is the step that surprises first-time raisers: 20 pounds live becomes 15 pounds dressed, a 75 to 78 percent yield. Size next year's flock on dressed weight, because that is the number that fills plates. The share of the meal you can grow puts that bird against the rest of the table.

Twelve Days Out: The Checklist

Adults at 1 pound, children under ten at half a pound, then round up to the nearest 2 pounds the store actually stocks. A party of 9 adults and 4 children is 11 pounds, so you buy a 12-pounder and accept that seconds are limited.

Next, frozen or fresh. Frozen costs $1.29 a pound and needs five days of fridge space, so it has to be in your freezer by the 20th. Fresh runs $2.99, keeps 1 to 2 days, and gets ordered now for pickup on the 24th or 25th.

Then the small things that ruin Thursday: a pan that does not fit, a rack that is missing, a bird still icy at the joint on Wednesday night. Check all three this week, and check the roasting time against your oven rather than the tag.

One more decision worth making early: whether the bird gets brined. That changes the shopping list by three cups of kosher salt and a five-gallon pail, and the salt for a 20-pound bird is measured off the weight you just chose. Stuffing runs three-quarters of a cup per pound, so an 18-pound bird takes 13 1/2 cups.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pounds of turkey do I need for 10 people?

A 12-pound bird. It yields about 5 pounds of cooked meat at 42 percent, which is 13 six-ounce plates. For leftovers on top of that, move up to a 15- or 16-pound bird, since a 12-pounder leaves less than a pound over.

Is it better to buy one 24-pound turkey or two 12-pound birds?

Two birds for anything over 20 people. A 24-pounder needs a 17-by-12-inch pan and two inches of oven clearance, roasts for five hours, and dries at the breast before the thigh is done. Two 12-pound birds finish in three hours and fit on two racks.

How much turkey per child?

Half a pound raw per child under ten, which lands about 3 ounces on the plate. Children rarely eat more than one pass at the white meat, and the extra pound you would have bought goes into the trash rather than the fridge.

Does a frozen turkey weigh more than a fresh one?

The number on the tag is the frozen weight, glaze included, and it is what you pay for. A 20-pound frozen bird is about 19 pounds of turkey and a pound of ice, so buy the same weight you would buy fresh and expect a slightly smaller yield.

The Numbers to Write Down

One pound per person, 1 1/2 with leftovers, 1 1/4 for birds under 12 pounds. A 20-pounder covers 20 at six ounces and 13 if you want the fridge stocked. Buy frozen by the 20th, start thawing on the 21st, and check the pan this week.

The exception is a table of big eaters. At eight ounces a plate with seconds, that 20-pound bird feeds 13, and buying to the lower number means carving thin.

The rest of the menu arithmetic sits in the same series: pounds of potatoes, stuffing by the cup, and gravy for twelve. Size the bird first. Everything else on the list follows from its weight.
