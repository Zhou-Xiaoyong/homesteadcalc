How Much Butter for Thanksgiving?

Published: November 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Twelve guests take 48 tablespoons of butter, which is 1 1/2 pounds. Buy two. The half pound left over is 16 tablespoons, and it goes into Friday's sandwiches and a second pan of rolls.

That is the sum of the dishes, not a portion chart, using quantities worked out here. Two weeks out, butter is the easy line. Cream, milk, and whether to churn your own decide the trip.

Forty-Eight Tablespoons, Counted Dish by Dish

Nothing here is optional except the butter under the turkey skin.

Dish Butter in the recipe Tablespoons
Mashed potatoes, 8 lb 1/2 cup 8
Stuffing, 15 cups of cubes 1 stick plus 2 tbsp 10
Dinner rolls, 36 1/2 cup 8
Pie crusts, two single 9-inch 1 cup 16
Turkey, under the breast skin 4 tbsp 4
Green beans or sprouts 2 tbsp 2
Total 1 1/2 lb 48

The potatoes are the Idaho Potato Commission ratio scaled four times: 2 pounds to 3/4 cup hot milk to 2 tablespoons of butter. Eight pounds is four batches: a half cup of butter and three cups of milk.

Stuffing runs half a cup per 12 cups of cubes, which puts 15 cups at 10 tablespoons. Rolls take a half cup for three dozen. Pie crust is the heavy line, a half cup per single shell.

Gravy is absent because it is built on the bird's fat. A compound butter for the table pushes the total to 56 tablespoons.

A Pound Is Four Sticks and Thirty-Two Tablespoons

One pound is four sticks, two cups, 32 tablespoons, 16 ounces. A tablespoon is half an ounce, about 14 grams. Butter is the one ingredient where every unit converts cleanly.

Forty-eight tablespoons is six sticks. The two left in the box are the buffer: the pan of rolls somebody drops, the roux that breaks, the pie for a cousin who arrives hungry.

The list can be written three ways and mean one thing: eight sticks, two pounds, four half-cup measures. Stores sell by the pound and recipes speak in sticks, and that mismatch is how hosts under-buy.

What the 80 Percent Rule Does to a Recipe

US butter must be at least 80 percent milkfat, with 16 to 18 percent water and a percent or two of milk solids. Two pounds is therefore 1.6 pounds of fat and five ounces of water, working for you in a batter and against you in a skillet.

Brown butter is the clearest case. The nutty flavor is milk solids toasting; the foam is water boiling off. Half a cup browned until it smells of hazelnut loses roughly 15 percent of its weight, so a recipe wanting half a cup browned needs nine and a half tablespoons raw. Brown first, then measure.

European-style butter at 82 to 85 percent fat changes two things. Crust gets flakier, because more fat and less water means less gluten. It also browns faster, since less water is left to hold the surface near 212 degrees while it evaporates. Drop the oven 25 degrees or shield the rim.

The Cream Is a Separate Trip

Item Needed for 12 What it does Buy
Unsalted butter 1 1/2 lb Potatoes, stuffing, rolls, crusts 2 lb
Heavy cream, 36% 1 pint Whipped cream for 16 slices 1 pint, or a quart if it goes in the potatoes
Whole milk 6 1/4 cups 3 cups potatoes, 3 1/4 cups roll dough 1/2 gallon

Sixteen slices at a quarter cup each is four cups. Heavy cream roughly doubles as it whips, so a pint of 36 percent yields four cups, enough and not a spoonful more.

Buy the quart if the pies go whole to the table, or if you swap cream for half the milk in the potatoes. Buy the pint if you plate.

Unstabilized cream on a pie that sits out weeps before the coffee is poured. A teaspoon of unflavored gelatin per cup, bloomed in cold water and melted, folded in at soft peaks, holds four hours at room temperature.

The milk line is the one nobody checks. Potatoes take three cups, the roll dough three and a quarter, so a half gallon leaves 1 3/4 cups spare.

Two pounds of finished butter takes about 2.4 quarts of 36 percent cream, because a quart gives 13 to 14 ounces once the buttermilk leaves. At $4.50 to $5.50 a quart that is $10.80 to $13.20, plus half an hour of churning.

Churning for the holiday costs a dollar or two more than the store brand and pays back five cups of buttermilk. A fine project. Not a way to save money on a meal for twelve.

The pick for this menu: two pounds of unsalted store brand. Salted is a table question, not a cooking one, and a crust written as a half cup of butter plus a half teaspoon of salt assumes unsalted.

Buy It Now or Buy It Tuesday

Butter holds six to nine months at 0 degrees Fahrenheit with no loss of performance, so the two weeks before Thanksgiving are a price question, not a freshness one. Buy on sale now, freeze, move it to the refrigerator Tuesday.

Wrap it. Butter takes the smell of everything else in the box within a month, and a chest freezer holding a pig will hand that flavor back through your crust. Foil plus a bag is enough.

Cream runs the other way. Pasteurized heavy cream carries a date two to three weeks out and should be bought within a week of Thursday. Ultra-pasteurized lasts six weeks but whips slower and coarser, and the 280 degree flash heat leaves it tasting flat.

Salt is the last decision. Salted butter carries about a quarter teaspoon per stick, so 48 tablespoons hides 1 1/2 teaspoons you never measured. Write the crust and the stuffing for unsalted and buy salted only for the bread basket. Going all salted moves the seasoning out of your hands.

If the Cream Comes From Your Own Animal

Two pounds of butter is roughly 4.4 gallons of Jersey milk at 4.8 percent butterfat, or 6.2 gallons of Holstein at 3.5. A Jersey at peak gives four to five gallons a day, so the holiday batch is a day of milk, twice that skimmed by hand.

Gravity skimming recovers 60 to 75 percent of the butterfat, so plan a third more milk. A separator gets about 95 percent. Either way, churn at 58 to 65 degrees: cold cream takes twice as long and leaves more fat in the buttermilk.

Where It Goes Wrong on Thursday

Three failures do most of the damage, and all three are temperature.

Crust. Butter goes in at 35 to 40 degrees, cut in until pea-sized, dough under 65. Warm butter smears instead of layering, and the shell bakes like a cracker.

Stuffing. Melt the butter; do not brown it. Browned butter reads bitter against dried bread and sage.

Whipped cream. Stop at soft to medium peaks. Cream goes glossy to grainy in ten seconds with no way back. If it happens, stir in a tablespoon of cold milk and stop beating.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much butter do I need for Thanksgiving for 12 people?

Forty-eight tablespoons, 1 1/2 pounds or six sticks, across the potatoes, stuffing, rolls, two crusts, and a little for the bird. Buy two pounds; the last half pound covers Friday's sandwiches.

Is it cheaper to make butter for Thanksgiving?

No. Two pounds takes about 2.4 quarts of 36 percent cream, which at $4.50 to $5.50 a quart is $10.80 to $13.20 against $9 to $11 for store brand. You pay a dollar or two for five cups of buttermilk.

How much heavy cream do I need for whipped cream for 12 people?

One pint. Sixteen slices at a quarter cup each is four cups, and heavy cream doubles as it whips, so a pint of 36 percent covers it with nothing to spare. Buy a quart if the pies go whole to the table.

Can I buy butter now and freeze it for Thanksgiving?

Yes. Butter holds six to nine months at 0 degrees Fahrenheit, so two weeks in the freezer costs nothing. Keep the foil on and bag it, because it takes the smell of whatever else is in the box.

The Numbers to Write Down

Forty-eight tablespoons, six sticks, two pounds bought. One pint of cream, a half gallon of milk, and 1 1/2 teaspoons of salt if all of it is salted. Store brand runs $9 to $11; churning your own runs $10.80 to $13.20 and gives back five cups of buttermilk.

The exception is a table that skips pie. Take out the crusts and the total is 32 tablespoons, one pound exactly, so a single box covers the meal with no buffer for the second pan of rolls.
