Tomato Spacing in Raised Beds: How Close Is Too Close?

Published: November 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Determinate tomatoes go 18 to 24 inches apart in a raised bed, in every direction. Indeterminate types need 30 to 36 inches, or 12 to 18 inches if you train each one to a single stem on a stake. A 4-by-8 bed holds 8 plants at 24 inches, 10 at 18, and 3 at 36.

The measurement that decides this is not the width of the transplant in your hand. It is the width the plant reaches in August. A tomato in a 4-inch pot is about 6 inches across. The same plant, caged and unpruned, is 3 to 4 feet across, and two of those at 24-inch centers overlap by more than a foot before the first fruit colors.

What a Raised Bed Changes

Three things, and only one of them is about spacing. The soil drains and warms earlier in spring, so roots colonize the whole bed instead of sitting in a cold pocket. Nobody walks on it, so it never compacts. And the width is fixed, usually 4 feet, because you have to reach the middle from both sides.

That last one is the constraint most spacing advice ignores. A 4-foot bed does not hold three rows at 18 inches, since 48 divided by 18 is 2.6. You get two columns and a foot of unused strip left over. This is why bed gardeners plant on a grid and stop thinking in rows altogether.

Depth counts too. Twelve inches is the floor for tomatoes and 18 is better, because roots want 3 to 4 feet of run in open ground and a shallow bed caps how far they go. The raised bed soil calculator turns depth into cubic feet, yards, and bag counts.

The Short Answer, by Type

Spacing follows growth habit, not variety names. Grid means equal distance in both directions, which is what a bed wants.

Indeterminate, caged and unpruned 30–36 in grid 2–3 5-ft cage
Indeterminate, one stem on a stake 18 in, rows 24 in apart 10 7-ft stake or string
Indeterminate, two stems 24 in grid 8 6-ft stake or weave

Dwarf and patio types 15–18 in grid 10–18 3-ft stake

Determinates stop on their own. They set a terminal bud, put their fruit in one concentrated window, and quit at 3 to 4 feet, which is why they tolerate 18 inches. Indeterminates keep building vine until frost, so their spacing has to be paid up front.

Why the Seed Packet Number Misleads You

A packet reading "space 24 inches apart in rows 36 inches apart" gives two numbers for two different jobs. The first is what the plant needs. The second is clearance for a tiller and a walking path.

In a bed, that 36 inches is 3 square feet of production handed to a path you will never use. The calculator shows the cost directly: tomatoes in a 4-by-8 bed come out at 4 plants on the row pattern and 8 on the grid pattern. Same soil, same seed, double the fruit.

Use the in-row number in both directions and ignore the second one. Row spacing earns its place in a field, where equipment has to pass and air has to move across long blocks of plants. Neither applies inside 32 square feet.

Canopy Width Is the Real Limit

A caged indeterminate in August is 5 to 6 feet tall and 3 to 4 feet across. At 24-inch centers, two neighbors share a foot of canopy, and the lowest leaves on both plants sit in still air for the rest of the season.

That overlap is harmless in July. It costs you in the third week of August, when the bottom leaves yellow, spot, and drop, and the fruit they were shading is suddenly exposed to sunscald.

Why one tomato per square foot is not wrong

Square foot gardening puts a single indeterminate in a 12-inch square, and the method works, because the plant is trained to one leader on a vertical support and every sucker gets pinched. Its footprint stays at the stake. Skip the weekly pruning and 12 inches becomes a disease nursery by mid-July. Spacing and training are one decision, not two.

What Crowding Actually Costs

Disease climbs up from the soil

Early blight and Septoria leaf spot both overwinter in last year's vines and travel on splashing water. The first 18 inches of the plant is where those spores land. Plants at 18 inches keep their lowest leaves under a neighbor's canopy. Plants at 36 inches do not. Two inches of straw mulch does more than any spray here, because it stops the splash at the soil line.

Late-season shade

A 5-foot plant at latitude 40°N throws a shadow about 1.5 feet long at noon in late June. By late September the noon sun sits 23 degrees lower and the same plant throws 4 feet of shade, nearly three times as far. Put the tall plants on the north edge of the bed: the shadow lands in the path and the south strip stays in full sun for lettuce or basil.

Two Layouts That Beat a Grid

Measure in from the edge

Most people set the first plant in the corner. Set it 12 inches in from the long edge instead and the second column lands 24 inches away with 12 inches left at the far edge. In a 4-foot bed that recovers the strip the grid math throws away, and it puts both columns within arm's reach of the path.

Offset the second column

Shift one column by half the spacing and the plants form triangles instead of squares. The distance between any two plants stays the same, so nothing is crowded, but the bed holds about 15% more. In a 4-by-8 bed at 24 inches, that is 8 plants becoming 9.

Depth and Water Change the Math

Twelve inches of soil under 8 plants is 4 cubic feet each. That is 32 cubic feet for the bed, 1.2 cubic yards, and roughly 16 bags of 2-cubic-foot mix. Go to 18 inches and each plant gets 6 cubic feet, which is the cheapest way to make tight spacing work: same footprint, more root room, less competition for moisture.

Water sets the ceiling. Tomatoes want 1 to 2 inches a week, and 1 inch over 32 square feet is 20 gallons, so a 4-by-8 bed drinks 20 to 40 gallons in July. Split across 8 plants that is 2.5 to 5 gallons each. Uneven moisture is also what turns into blossom-end rot: the calcium is usually in the soil already, but the plant cannot move it into the fruit while the moisture swings.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far apart should tomatoes be in a raised bed?

18 to 24 inches for determinate types, measured in both directions. Indeterminates need 30 to 36 inches unless they are trained to a single stem, which allows 18 inches in the row with 24 inches between rows.

Can I plant tomatoes 12 inches apart?

Only with a single stem and a stake. At 12 inches there is no room for a cage, so the plant has to be pruned to one leader every week and tied to a 7-foot support. Skip the pruning for three weeks in July and the canopy closes.

How many tomato plants fit in a 4x8 raised bed?

Eight at 24-inch spacing, 10 at 18 inches, and 2 to 3 at 36 inches. For indeterminates grown in cages, plan on 3 plants in a bed that size.

Does pruning let me plant tomatoes closer together?

Pruning changes the canopy width, not the root spread. A single-stem plant at 18 inches still sends roots under its neighbor, so water and fertilizer have to cover both. Tighter planting works only when the bed is deep and the drip line is shared on purpose.

The Numbers to Carry Outside

18 to 24 inches for determinates, 30 to 36 for caged indeterminates, 18 inches in the row for single-stem plants with 24 between rows. A 4-by-8 bed takes 8 at 24 inches and 10 at 18. Twelve inches of soil minimum, 32 cubic feet per bed, 20 to 40 gallons of water a week in July.

The exception is cherry types and anything sold as vigorous: give them the wide end even when staked, because they outgrow the number by August. If the bed backs onto a south wall, add 6 inches to every figure, since reflected heat and blocked airflow both push disease pressure up.
