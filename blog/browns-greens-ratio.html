Browns to Greens: The Easy Volume Ratio for Compost

Published: August 13, 2026 · 9 min read

If you remember only one number for composting, make it this: aim for about 2 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume—anywhere from a 2:1 to a 3:1 ratio. That simple bucket-to-bucket balance lands you close to the 25:1 to 30:1 carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio by weight that soil microbes need to break material down quickly and without odor. You don't need a scale, a chemistry degree, or a perfect formula—just a consistent container and a feel for what counts as a brown versus a green.

The hard part isn't the math—it's that the weight ratio people quote (about 30:1 C:N) is very different from the volume ratio you measure by hand (about 2:1 or 3:1 browns to greens). In this guide we'll explain why, give you a dead-simple bucket method, and show how to rescue a lopsided pile. When you want exact ratios from your own materials, use our Compost Mix Calculator.

Why the Volume Ratio Isn't the Same as the Weight Ratio

Textbooks and extension services talk about compost balance in terms of carbon-to-nitrogen by weight: about 30 parts carbon for every 1 part nitrogen. That's the sweet spot where decomposer microbes have enough energy (carbon) and enough protein-building material (nitrogen) to multiply fast and heat up the pile.

But here's the catch: a "brown" and a "green" weigh wildly different amounts for the same bucket. Dry fall leaves are fluffy and weigh almost nothing—a full 5-gallon bucket of shredded leaves might tip the scale at 1–2 lbs yet carries a massive amount of carbon (around 60:1 C:N). Fresh grass clippings, by contrast, are dense and wet; that same 5-gallon bucket can weigh 8–10 lbs and is nitrogen-rich (around 17:1 C:N).

So if you matched them one-for-one by volume, the heavy greens would swamp the light browns and your pile would turn nitrogen-heavy, slimy, and smelly. To hit the 30:1 C:N ratio by weight, you have to add more browns by volume. That's why the practical rule is 2 to 3 buckets of browns for every 1 bucket of greens.

The takeaway: C:N is by weight (target ~30:1). The browns-to-greens ratio is by volume (target 2:1 to 3:1). They describe the same balanced pile from two different measuring cups.

The Bucket Method: 2 to 3 Parts Browns per 1 Part Greens

Grab any single container—a 5-gallon bucket, a trug, even a shovel-load—and call it "one part." For every one part of greens you add, throw in two to three parts of browns. Here's what happens across the range:

By Volume (browns : greens) Approx. C:N by Weight What to Expect
1:1 (equal parts) ~15–20:1 Nitrogen-heavy; can go slimy and smell of ammonia
2:1 ~25–30:1 Ideal—fast, hot, low odor
3:1 ~30–40:1 Slightly carbon-heavy; a bit slower but very forgiving
4:1 or more 40+:1 Too carbon-heavy; cold, slow, barely shrinks

Most beginners do best starting at 2:1 if they turn the pile regularly, or 3:1 if they dump a lot of wet kitchen scraps and don't turn often. Both sit comfortably inside the healthy window. The 3:1 version simply leaves more carbon buffer so an extra bucket of carrot peels won't tip the pile into stink.

A quick visual guide to "brown" and "green"

Browns (carbon-rich): dry leaves, straw, hay, shredded newspaper and cardboard, sawdust, wood chips, pine needles, corn stalks, and paper towel rolls. They're typically dry, fibrous, and brown or tan.

Greens (nitrogen-rich): grass clippings, vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds and filters, fresh garden weeds, tea leaves, manure from chickens, cows, or horses, and spent annual flowers. They're moist, often green, and break down fast. Notice that coffee grounds are a green even though they look brown—color is not the rule, C:N is.

How to Measure by Volume (No Scale Required)

The whole point of the volume method is that it's foolproof. Three habits make it work:

  • Use one consistent container. Always measure "parts" with the same bucket or tub so a part stays a part.
  • Layer like lasagna. Start with a 4–6 inch layer of coarse browns (twigs, straw) for airflow, then alternate a 2–3 inch layer of greens with a thicker 4–6 inch layer of browns. Each green layer gets a browns "lid" to absorb moisture and keep flies away.
  • Shred and chop. Run leaves over a mower, rip cardboard into strips, and chop big vegetable chunks. Smaller pieces break down faster and pack more evenly, so your volume estimate stays honest.

If your pile is in a tumbler, just alternate handfuls: two scoops of leaves or straw for every one scoop of scraps. It really is that simple.

How Common Materials Behave by Volume

Not every brown fills the same space, and not every green settles the same way. Knowing the quirks keeps your volume estimate accurate:

Material Brown / Green Volume Behavior & Tip
Dry leaves Brown Shred first; a big bag compresses to surprisingly little once wet
Straw Brown Very bulky and airy; great structure, slow to break down
Sawdust Brown Packs dense and is extremely high C:N—use thin layers only
Shredded cardboard Brown Remove tape and glossy print; fluff it so it doesn't mat
Grass clippings Green Heavy and wet; spread thin or it mats and goes anaerobic
Vegetable scraps Green Chop small; they add both nitrogen and moisture
Coffee grounds Green Dense and nitrogen-rich despite the dark color; counts as a green
Manure Green Very "hot"; balance each part with 2–3 parts browns
Rule of thumb: If a material is dry and you could light it (leaves, straw, paper, wood), it's a brown—use more of it by volume. If it's fresh, moist, or came out of the garden or kitchen, it's a green—use less by volume.

Fixing a Lopsided Pile

Even with the bucket method, piles drift. The good news is the symptoms are obvious and the fixes are the same every time.

Too many greens (wet, smelly, slimy): You'll get an ammonia or rotten-egg smell and a soggy texture. Add a thick layer of browns—shredded leaves, straw, or torn cardboard—and turn the pile to pull in oxygen. The smell usually fades within a day.

Too many browns (cold, dry, slow): The pile barely warms, stays dry, and the materials look intact weeks later. Add greens and a little water, then turn. A pile needs both nitrogen and moisture to fire up the microbes.

A compost thermometer takes the guesswork out of "is it working?": a healthy hot pile runs 130–150°F in the center within a few days of building, which is also hot enough to kill most weed seeds and pathogens.

A Simple Weekly Routine That Stays Balanced

You don't need to obsess over every scrap. A routine keeps the ratio steady:

  1. Keep a covered tote of shredded dry leaves or a bale of straw next to the bin as your "brown bank."
  2. Collect greens (kitchen scraps, grass) through the week in a countertop bin.
  3. Each time you empty a container of greens into the pile, top it with two to three containers of browns.
  4. Turn the pile every 1–2 weeks, or give a tumbler a few spins every time you add material.
  5. Check moisture with the wrung-out sponge test: damp, not dripping.

Putting It Together

The browns-to-greens ratio by volume is the most useful shortcut in all of composting: 2 to 3 buckets of browns for every 1 bucket of greens, measured with the same container, layered and turned. That single habit gets you to the 25–30:1 C:N ratio the microbes want—without weighing a thing. Let smell and heat be your feedback: ammonia means more browns, cold-and-dry means more greens, and earthy-and-warm means you've nailed it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 2:1 or a 3:1 browns to greens ratio better?

Both work. A 2:1 ratio runs hotter and finishes faster because it sits right around the 25–30:1 C:N sweet spot. A 3:1 ratio is slightly more carbon-heavy, so it decomposes a little slower but is very forgiving and almost never smells. Choose 2:1 if you turn often and want speed; choose 3:1 if you add lots of wet kitchen scraps and want a safety margin.

Can I use a 1:1 browns to greens ratio?

Not by volume. A 1:1 mix by volume is nitrogen-heavy (roughly 15–20:1 C:N by weight) and tends to go slimy and smell of ammonia because the greens outpace the browns' ability to absorb excess moisture and nitrogen. Stick to at least 2 parts browns per 1 part greens by volume.

Why are coffee grounds considered a green if they are brown?

Color is misleading. Compost "greens" and "browns" are defined by carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, not appearance. Used coffee grounds run about 20:1 C:N, which makes them nitrogen-rich—a green. They are dark in color but should be counted as a nitrogen material when balancing your pile.

How do I know if my browns to greens ratio is off?

Your senses tell you. A pile that smells like ammonia, rotten eggs, or is wet and slimy has too many greens—add browns and turn it. A pile that stays cold, dry, and barely shrinks has too many browns—add greens and water. A healthy, balanced pile heats to 130–150°F and smells like earth.

Do I need to weigh my compost materials?

No. The bucket method—measuring by volume with the same container—gets you close enough for a working pile. Weighing only matters if you want to hit an exact C:N ratio, which our Compost Mix Calculator can do for you from the materials you have on hand.
