Canning Leftover Turkey: How Many Pints From One Bird?

Published: November 16, 2026 · 9 min read

Nine pints, four and a half quarts, or a mix of both. A 20-pound turkey gives up 9 pounds of cooked meat, a wide-mouth pint packs about a pound of it, and the whole batch processes in a single 75-minute load.

The jars are the part to buy this week. Everything below is per bird and per jar at 2026 prices, and it assumes a roasted bird that was never stuffed and never dropped in a fryer.

What One Bird Fills

Cooked meat runs 42 to 48 percent of dressed weight, climbing with the size of the frame because a bigger bird carries proportionally more breast. A wide-mouth pint holds roughly a pound of cubed meat topped off with broth, and a quart holds about two.

Dressed weight Cooked meat Wide-mouth pints Quarts
12 lb 5.0 lb 5 2 1/2
16 lb 7.0 lb 7 3 1/2
20 lb 9.0 lb 9 4 1/2
24 lb 11.5 lb 11 5 1/2

Pints are the better size for two. A quart is a lot of turkey to finish inside four days, and nothing improves in the refrigerator.

The frame is a second batch. A 20-pound carcass, skin, and pan drippings give 3 to 4 quarts of stock, which is 6 to 8 pints, and stock runs 20 minutes for pints and 25 for quarts against 75 and 90 for meat. Process it in its own load. Stacking stock in with the meat works, but the whole canner runs to 90 minutes and the pints come out safe and soft.

75 Minutes at Pressure, and 1 1/4 Inches of Headspace

Turkey is low-acid, pH above 4.6, so a water bath cannot make it safe no matter how long it boils. The number that matters is 240 degrees Fahrenheit, and only pressure gets water that hot. If that rule is new to you, start with which foods need which canner before you buy anything.

Headspace is 1 1/4 inches, not the half inch you use for jam. Meat and broth both expand against the lid, and a jar filled to the shoulder will push liquid into the canner water and seal short.

A hot pack means hot meat under hot broth, not meat cooked twice. Starting the jar warm instead of cold is the difference between a clean pint and a jar of grey meat.

Jar Minutes Dial gauge Weighted gauge
Pint, meat 75 11 lb 10 lb
Quart, meat 90 11 lb 10 lb
Pint, stock 20 11 lb 10 lb
Quart, stock 25 11 lb 10 lb

Those pressures hold from sea level to 1,000 feet on a weighted gauge. Above that, a weighted gauge goes to 15 pounds and stays there, while a dial climbs one pound per 2,000 feet: 12 at 2,000 to 4,000, 13 at 4,000 to 6,000, 14 above 6,000. A dial gauge drifts over time, so have it checked at the county extension office once a year. The test is free and takes ten minutes.

Two habits shorten the evening. Vent steam for a full ten minutes before you set the weight, and let the canner come down to zero on its own. A quick-release or a cold towel on the lid pulls liquid out of the jars and leaves you with half-full pints that look fine and are not.

Fill the canner to the line in the manual, usually 2 to 3 inches. A 90-minute quart load boils off most of an inch, and a canner that goes dry warps its bottom.

The Shopping List

A 23-quart canner takes 7 quarts or 16 pints, so 9 pints from a 20-pound bird leaves the second rack free for stock. If your canner is a 16-quart, plan two loads and an extra hour.

Per pint, the running cost is 42 cents for a lid, about a nickel of amortized jar, and 4 cents of electricity for two hours on the burner. Call it 51 cents. The jars are the only real outlay, and they come back every year until you drop one on the cellar floor.

Two dozen pints looks like overbuying for one bird. It is not. Nine jars hold the meat, eight hold the stock, and the rest are waiting when the December pork comes back from the processor.

Buy new lids. A lid that sealed once has a compressed gasket and will not seat a second time, and the failure shows up in February as a jar of turkey with a bulging top. Skip the decorative antique jars for pressure work. Old glass with heavy shoulders and an unknown history is a poor gamble on a quart of turkey.

What Cannot Go in the Jar

Some of Thanksgiving cannot be canned, and the list is short enough to memorize:

  • Stuffing. Bread dressing is dense and wet, heat will not reach the middle on any tested schedule, and no university has published a safe process for it.
  • Gravy thickened with flour or cornstarch. Starch insulates the cold spot. Can the stock plain and thicken it when you open the jar, which is the same arithmetic the gravy-per-person numbers use.
  • Anything that sat out past two hours, including the platter that went back and forth to the table four times.
  • Plate scrapings and picked-over bones. What comes off a guest's plate is not canning stock.
  • Turkey salad, casseroles, and soup with noodles or rice. Mayo, dairy, and pasta have no approved process at all.

The window is tighter than people expect. Get the meat into the refrigerator within two hours of carving, and get it into jars within three or four days. Cook Thursday, can Sunday. Waiting until the following weekend means the meat has already been in the fridge past the point where the USDA says it is still good, and canning does not rescue food that was already old.

Canning or Freezing the Rest

Nine pounds of cooked meat takes 0.36 cubic feet of freezer space at 0.04 cubic feet per pound, a third of the shelf the bird itself needed before the holiday. That math is in the freezer-space guide.

The freezer wins on effort and loses on time. Cooked turkey holds two to three months frozen before the texture goes stringy. Canned, it holds a year at full quality and does not care whether the power comes back on. A chest freezer costs about $8 a month to run, so a third of a cubic foot is 45 cents a month, and canning overtakes it somewhere around month four.

Size the bird before you size the jars. If 9 pounds of leftovers is more than you will eat by March, a 16-pound bird gives 7 pints and no guilt, and the pounds-per-person math gets you there from the guest count.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you pressure can leftover turkey from Thanksgiving?

Yes, and it is the only safe way to keep it on a shelf. Pack the cooked meat into jars with hot broth, leave 1 1/4 inches of headspace, and process pints 75 minutes and quarts 90 minutes at 10 pounds on a weighted gauge or 11 pounds on a dial gauge near sea level. Refrigerate the meat the day it is cooked and get it into jars within three or four days.

How many pints does a 20-pound turkey make?

About nine. A 20-pound dressed bird yields 9 pounds of cooked meat at 45 percent, and a wide-mouth pint holds roughly a pound of cubed meat and broth. Packed into quarts instead, the same meat fills four and a half jars. The frame adds another 3 to 4 quarts of stock, which is 6 to 8 pints processed separately for 20 minutes.

How long does home-canned turkey last?

Twelve months at best quality, and longer than that if the jars stay between 50 and 70 degrees Fahrenheit in the dark. Lids that have lost their seal, hissed on the counter, or left a streak of dried liquid under the ring go in the trash rather than the pot. Boil low-acid canned meat for 10 minutes before you taste it.

Do you add water when canning turkey?

Not water. Cover the meat with hot turkey broth and leave 1 1/4 inches of headspace, because meat and liquid both expand as the jar heats. A raw pack goes in without added liquid at all, since raw meat releases its own juice, but raw pack takes a pound of uncooked meat per pint, which is a different job from putting up a roasted bird.

The Numbers to Write Down

Nine pints from a 20-pounder, 75 minutes at 10 pounds, 1 1/4 inches of headspace, nine new lids at $3.80. Add the stock load at 20 minutes a pint and the evening is over in about three hours.

The exception is a bird that spent the afternoon on the table. Two hours out of the refrigerator is the line, and past it the answer is the freezer or the bin, not the canner.
