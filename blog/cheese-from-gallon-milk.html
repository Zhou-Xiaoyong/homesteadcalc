How Much Cheese From a Gallon of Milk? By Cheese Type

Published: September 21, 2026 · 9 min read

One gallon of milk makes about 1.3 to 1.7 pounds of fresh cheese, 0.85 to 1 pound of mozzarella or feta, 0.7 to 0.85 pounds of cheddar, and 0.5 to 0.7 pounds of parmesan. Mozzarella is the figure most people have heard — one pound from one gallon — and it sits right in the middle of the range. Ricotta nearly doubles it; aged grating cheese nearly halves it.

The reason is not the milk. Every one of those cheeses starts from the same 8.6 pounds of liquid, roughly 87 percent of which is water. What changes is how much of that water you deliberately leave in the finished wheel. The tables below give the numbers by style and by milk source, and the sections after them explain where the yield leaks away. Cheese Yield From One Gallon of Milk, by Type

A gallon of cow milk weighs about 8.6 pounds, so a yield percentage and a pound figure are the same statement in different units. Yield percentage here means finished cheese weight divided by the weight of the milk that went in.

Cheese Typical Milk Yield % Per Gallon Approx. Cups
Ricotta (whole milk) Cow, goat, or sheep 15–20% 1.3–1.7 lb 2.5–3.5 cups
Cottage cheese Cow (skim or 2%) 12–16% 1.0–1.4 lb curd 2–3 cups
Chèvre Goat 14–18% 1.2–1.5 lb 2.5–3 cups
Queso fresco / paneer Cow 12–15% 1.0–1.3 lb 2–2.5 cups
Feta (brined) Cow or sheep 11–14% 0.95–1.2 lb 2–2.75 cups
Brie / Camembert Cow 10–13% 0.85–1.1 lb 2–2.5 cups
Mozzarella (fresh) Cow 10–12% 0.85–1.0 lb 3.5–4 cups shredded
Gouda / Monterey Jack Cow 9–11% 0.8–0.95 lb 3.5–4 cups shredded
Cheddar Cow 8–10% 0.7–0.85 lb 3–3.5 cups shredded
Parmesan / Romano (aged) Cow or sheep 6–8% 0.5–0.7 lb 2.5–3 cups grated

Notice how tight the bands are within a style and how wide they are across styles. That is not sloppiness: a cheesemaker targeting cheddar can hit 9 percent reliably, but the same recipe pushed to 11 percent would be a rubbery, short-lived wheel.

Why the Same Gallon Gives 0.7 Pounds or 1.7

Moisture does most of the work

A hard cheese is drained and pressed down to about 37 percent moisture; parmesan is aged to about 30 percent; fresh chèvre comes out of the bag at 60 to 70 percent, and ricotta higher still. Work through the arithmetic and the whole yield table falls out of that one number. Two identical cheeses from identical milk will differ by 15 percent in finished weight if one is drained six hours longer than the other.

Fat and casein set the ceiling

Cow milk averages 3.5 percent fat and 3.25 percent protein per USDA food data, with about 80 percent of that protein being casein — the fraction that actually becomes cheese. Fat plus casein therefore comes to roughly 6 percent of the milk's weight, so parmesan at 6 to 8 percent is close to the physical limit. Everything above that line is water and salt you chose to keep, which is exactly why cheddar presses out more whey than gouda.

Acid decides how much calcium stays

As the culture acidifies the vat, calcium and phosphate dissolve out of the curd and leave with the whey, carrying protein along with them. Cheddar and parmesan are acid-driven cheeses and pay for their flavor with yield. Brie and camembert are rennet-set with very little acid development, which is one reason a soft-ripened wheel gets more cheese out of the same gallon.

Cut size and cook temperature fine-tune the rest

Cutting curd into smaller cubes and cooking it hotter both squeeze out more whey. A quarter-inch cut cooked at 102°F leaves a moist, high-yield curd; a rice-grain cut taken to 110°F gives a drier one. Neither is better — they are different cheeses.

Milk Source Shifts Yield Before You Start

All the numbers above assume ordinary Holstein cow milk at 3.5 percent fat. Change the animal or the breed and the yield moves before you have touched the vat:

  • Jersey and Guernsey cows: 4.5 to 5 percent butterfat — expect 10 to 15 percent more cheese per gallon than the same recipe on Holstein milk.
  • Goat milk: 3.5 to 4 percent fat with smaller fat globules, so the curd is delicate and sheds fines easily. Great yield per gallon, harder to handle. Details are in the goat stocking-rate guide if you are sizing a herd to feed the vat.
  • Sheep milk: 6 to 7 percent fat and 5.5 percent protein. The same gallon gives roughly 50 percent more cheese than cow milk and about 1.2 to 1.5 pounds of fresh feta or ricotta-style cheese.

Working Backwards: Milk Needed for One Pound

Most people plan the other direction — from a recipe that calls for a pound — so here is the ladder inverted:

Finished Cheese Milk Required Notes
1 lb ricotta 2–2.5 quarts Yields roughly 7–8 cups of fresh cheese
1 lb chèvre 2.5–3 quarts goat milk Drained 12–24 hours; time sets the firmness
1 lb paneer or queso fresco 2.5–3 quarts Acid-set, pressed 30–60 minutes
1 lb fresh mozzarella 1 gallon The classic 30-minute recipe ratio
1 lb cheddar or gouda 1.2–1.4 gallons Pressed 12 hours at 20–30 psi equivalent
2 lb cheddar wheel 2.5–3 gallons Fits a 2-lb mold from a 6–8 qt pot
1 lb parmesan or romano 1.5–2 gallons Aged 10–24 months; large weight loss in the cave

What Happens to the Other 87 Percent

Whey is not waste, but it is not cheese either. A gallon of milk leaves roughly 3.5 quarts of sweet whey behind, and recooking it with a splash of vinegar or citric acid recovers another 4 to 8 ounces of ricotta — about half a cup to a cup. Add a quart of fresh milk to the whey and the return roughly doubles. Beyond that, whey goes into bread dough in place of water, into brine for feta and mozzarella, into soups and oatmeal, or out to the chickens and the compost pile. It is acidic and salty, so keep it off garden beds you intend to plant lime-loving crops in.

Is Home Cheese Actually Cheaper Than Store Cheese?

Rarely, if you are counting only dollars. A gallon of raw milk at $8 to $10 yields about 0.8 pounds of cheddar, so the milk alone costs $10 to $12.50 per pound — more than commodity cheddar at the supermarket. The math flips in three situations: you keep a milk animal and the milk is effectively free, you buy milk in bulk at commodity prices, or you make fresh cheese, where 2.5 quarts of milk produces a pound of chèvre that sells for $12 to $16. Aged cheese is a flavor and craft purchase, not a savings plan, and honesty about that saves a lot of disappointment on the first wheel.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much cheese does one gallon of milk make?

It depends on the style. A gallon makes 1.3 to 1.7 pounds of fresh cheese such as ricotta, queso fresco, or chèvre; 0.85 to 1 pound of mozzarella or feta; 0.7 to 0.85 pounds of cheddar or gouda; and 0.5 to 0.7 pounds of parmesan or romano. The gap is water, not milk.

Why does a gallon of milk make more ricotta than cheddar?

Because ricotta keeps far more water. A gallon of milk weighs about 8.6 pounds, so a 9 percent cheddar yield is 0.77 pounds while an 18 percent fresh-cheese yield is 1.55 pounds. Cheddar is pressed and aged down to roughly 37 percent moisture; ricotta is scooped straight from the pot at 70 percent or more.

How much cheese does a gallon of goat milk make?

About 1.2 to 1.5 pounds of fresh chèvre, or roughly 0.9 to 1 pound of pressed goat cheese. Goat milk runs slightly higher in fat and protein than Holstein cow milk, but the curd is more fragile, so expect to lose a little more to fines in the whey. The Goat Cheese Yield Calculator handles the goat-specific numbers.

How many gallons of milk does it take to make a pound of cheese?

About 2 quarts for a pound of ricotta or chèvre, 1 gallon for a pound of mozzarella, 1.2 to 1.4 gallons for a pound of cheddar, and 1.5 to 2 gallons for a pound of parmesan. The harder and drier the cheese, the more milk each pound represents.

Can you make cheese from ultra-pasteurized milk?

Not rennet-set cheese. Ultra-pasteurization heats milk to about 280°F and leaves a curd too weak to cut, stir, and drain properly, so cheddar, mozzarella, and brie made from it turn out soupy. It works fine for acid-set styles such as ricotta, paneer, and queso fresco. If you are stuck with pasteurized or homogenized milk, ¼ teaspoon of calcium chloride per gallon restores most of the curd firmness.
