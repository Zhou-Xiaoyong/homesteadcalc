How Many Goats Per Acre? Stocking Rates by Forage

Published: September 14, 2026 · 9 min read

Plan on 2 to 4 goats per acre if the pasture has to feed them year-round, 4 to 6 goats per acre if you will feed hay part of the year, and no more than 10 even on intensively managed, fertilized pasture with a real rotation. The number is not fixed by breed or rulebook — it is set by how many pounds of forage your acres actually grow, how much of that you can harvest before the plants are damaged, and how much of the goats' diet comes out of a bale instead of the ground.

That is why two neighbors with identical acreage can honestly report different answers. This guide works the number out from both sides — the animal and the plant — and the Land Requirement Calculator rolls pasture, garden, orchard, and livestock space into a single property total.

Quick Answer: Goats Per Acre by Forage Type

Most extension guidance clusters in the same band once you read the fine print about whether hay is being fed. Use the row that matches your ground and your management, not the most optimistic number on the list:

Forage & Management Goats Per Acre What That Number Assumes
Continuous grazing, unimproved grass 1–3 No rotation, no hay. Overgrazing and parasite pressure cap the herd.
Rotated mixed pasture, hay in winter 4–6 3–6 paddocks, hay or browse covers 3–6 months of the year.
Rotated, limed and fertilized cool-season pasture 6–10 High-yield improved pasture, 21–30 day rest, supplemental hay still likely.
Wooded browse / brushland 3–5 Browse regenerates slowly; goats still need grass and minerals alongside it.
Arid rangeland / dryland pasture 1–3 Rainfall-limited. Stock for the dry year, not the wet one.
Pasture only, no hay ever bought 2–4 Good improved pasture, rotational grazing, and a willingness to sell in drought.

The same acre supports three goats or eight depending on whether you rotate, fertilize, and buy hay. Numbers that ignore those three variables are guesses.

Start With What One Goat Eats

Every stocking rate is downstream of dry matter intake. Goats eat a percentage of their body weight in dry matter each day, and that percentage swings widely with body size and stage of production — a dry doe at maintenance and a doe milking twins are not remotely the same animal on the pasture.

Class of Goat Typical Weight Dry Matter Intake Forage Per Day Animal Units
Dry doe or wether 130–150 lb 2.5–3.0% of body weight 3.5–4.5 lb 0.20
Doe with kids / lactating 150–175 lb 4.0–4.5% of body weight 6.0–7.5 lb 0.30
Weaned kid, growing 50–75 lb 3.5–4.0% of body weight 2.0–2.8 lb 0.15
Mature buck 170–200 lb 2.5–3.0% of body weight 4.5–5.5 lb 0.20–0.25
Miniature breed doe 50–75 lb 2.5–3.5% of body weight 1.5–2.5 lb 0.08–0.12

These come from standard extension forage-demand tables, where a goat's animal-unit equivalent is listed as 0.17 to 0.20, a doe with kids at 0.30, and a weaned kid at 0.15. Texas A&M puts goat intake at 4.0 to 4.5 percent of body weight; mid-Atlantic and Kentucky extension materials use 2.5 to 3 percent for mature animals at maintenance. Both are right — different animals, different seasons. Milking herds sit at the top of every range here; wethers kept for brush control at the bottom.

Annualized across maintenance, pregnancy, and lactation, a standard goat averages about 4 to 5 pounds of dry matter a day — 1,500 to 1,800 pounds a year, the same figure behind our guide to how much hay a goat eats per day. Then Ask What Your Acre Actually Grows

The other half of the equation is yield, and this is where most homesteaders overestimate their land. Managed cool-season pasture produces roughly 3 to 4 tons of dry matter per acre per year; unimproved, continuously grazed ground often produces under 1.5 tons. You can only harvest part of that — extension guidance for rotational systems uses a 50 percent utilization rate as the planning default, rising toward 70 percent in an intensive system where paddocks are fully rested.

Pasture Quality Annual Dry Matter Yield Usable at 50% Utilization Feeds (Pasture Only)
Poor, overgrazed, weedy 1,000–2,000 lb/acre 500–1,000 lb/acre Under 1 goat per acre
Average mixed grass 3,000–5,000 lb/acre 1,500–2,500 lb/acre 1–1.5 goats per acre
Good managed cool-season pasture 6,000–8,000 lb/acre 3,000–4,000 lb/acre 1.5–2.5 goats per acre
Excellent, fertilized and irrigated 9,000–12,000 lb/acre 4,500–6,000 lb/acre 2.5–3.5 goats per acre

The last column is the one that surprises people. An acre of good pasture feeds one and a half to two goats with no bought feed — not six. A 150-pound doe at an annual average of 4.5 pounds of dry matter a day eats about 1,650 pounds a year, nearer 2,000 with waste, and an acre of good pasture hands you only 3,000 to 4,000 usable pounds. So "4 to 6 goats per acre" is not a pasture-only figure at all — it is a pasture-plus-hay figure: four to six goats off the acre, plus a winter's worth of bales.

Rotation Is The Biggest Single Lever You Control

Rotational grazing changes the answer more than fertilizer, seed, or breed. Continuous grazing on one big field lets goats graze their favorites to the dirt, ignore the rest, and reinfect themselves with parasite larvae that hatch from dung pats and climb the grass. Rotation forces more even use and starves that cycle.

The mechanics are simple: divide the pasture into four to eight paddocks with portable electric netting or polywire, move the herd when forage is grazed to about 4 inches, and rest each paddock 21 to 30 days in fast spring growth, 30 to 45 days in summer. Penn State's meat goat guidance recommends moving goats every four to five days and never grazing below 4 inches — the practical way to cut internal parasite pressure without blanket deworming. Stocking rate also affects animal performance and pasture condition simultaneously, so pushing the rate costs you on both sides at once.

Multi-species grazing is worth knowing if you keep cattle or horses too: goats prefer browse and forbs, cattle prefer grass, so diets overlap only about half. Running cattle through a paddock after the goats cleans up goat parasite larvae and uses forage the goats left standing.

Browse, Brush, and Marginal Land

Goats are browsers, not grazers. Given the choice they take half or more of their diet from shrubs, brambles, and tree leaves, which is why wooded and brushy ground routinely supports 3 to 5 goats per acre despite poor grass yield — the browse layer is doing the feeding.

Two cautions come with that. Browse regenerates slowly, so wooded paddocks need longer rest than grass paddocks or get stripped to bare stems. And browse is seasonal: deciduous leaves are gone by November, which means your acreage is set by the worst month of the year, not the best. If you plan to run goats on scrub permanently, the perimeter matters as much as the forage — our Fencing Material Calculator sizes posts, wire, and corners from the perimeter length.

Standard Versus Miniature Breeds

Breed size is the fastest way to change goats per acre without moving a fence post. A Nigerian Dwarf or Pygmy doe weighs 50 to 75 pounds; a Saanen or Boer doe can weigh 200 to 250. Forage demand tracks body weight almost linearly, so the same acre supports roughly twice as many miniatures — 8 to 12 per acre on rotated mixed pasture against 4 to 6 standards. What does not shrink is parasite pressure and hoof damage, so on heavy clay or steep ground the soil sets the ceiling before forage does.

Signs You Are Overstocked

Stocking rates are a hypothesis, not a verdict. Walk the pasture in July and again in late September:

  • Bare ground between plants that stays bare — you are grazing below the 4-inch residual.
  • Weeds increasing while good species thin out. Selective grazing plus overstocking converts pasture to weeds.
  • Does going into winter thin. Condition falling on unchanged acreage means the land is not carrying them.
  • Fecal egg counts climbing and dosing failing. Barber pole worm tracks high stocking density closely.
  • Hay feeding starting earlier each year. A creeping calendar is the clearest signal the herd outgrew the ground.

Adding paddocks and lengthening rest is the cheapest fix, since it raises the effective capacity of the same acreage. If that is not enough, cut the herd or buy more hay. Frequently Asked Questions

How many goats per acre is realistic?

For most homesteads, 4 to 6 goats per acre of mixed pasture — assuming you feed hay in winter, drought, and any month the grass stops growing. Pasture-only with no hay, drop to 2 to 4. On poor, dry, or continuously grazed ground, 1 to 3.

Can you keep 2 goats on half an acre?

Yes, but half an acre will not feed them. Two standard goats need roughly 9 to 12 pounds of dry matter a day between them, which half an acre of average pasture cannot supply year-round. Budget hay for most of the year.

How many goats is one animal unit?

One animal unit is 1,000 pounds of live weight. Extension guidelines place five to six goats at one animal unit, and a goat's animal-unit equivalent runs 0.17 to 0.20. A dry 140-pound doe is about 0.2; a doe nursing kids closer to 0.3.

How much land do I need for 10 goats?

With hay supplementation, plan 2 to 2.5 acres of rotated pasture for 10 standard goats, plus secure fencing, a dry lot, and 10 to 12 square feet of shelter each. Pasture-only, figure 4 to 5 acres of good improved pasture.

How many miniature goats can I keep per acre?

Nigerian Dwarf and Pygmy goats weigh 50 to 75 pounds, so the same acre supports about twice as many: 8 to 12 miniatures per acre on rotated mixed pasture, 4 to 6 if pasture must feed them all year.
