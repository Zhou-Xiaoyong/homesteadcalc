How Many Apples for a Gallon of Cider?

Published: August 27, 2026 · 9 min read

You need about 30–40 medium apples to make one gallon of cider. In weight terms that is roughly 12–15 pounds of apples, or about one-third of a bushel. A standard 5-gallon batch therefor calls for around 2 bushels (150–175 lbs) of fruit. Those are good planning numbers, but the exact count depends on apple size, variety, ripeness, and how efficient your press is. Below is the full breakdown, plus a link to our Cider Making Calculator if you want to size a batch by the pound.

The Short Answer by Apple Size

The single most useful rule of thumb for home cidermakers is that one gallon of cider needs about a third of a bushel of apples. Because apples vary so much in size, here is what that means in actual fruit counts.

Apple Size Apples per Pound Apples per Gallon (≈13 lbs) Bushels per Gallon
Small (golf-ball) 3–4 40–50 0.31
Medium 2–3 30–40 0.31
Large 1.5–2 20–26 0.31
Average (mixed) 2.5 33 0.31

Notice that the bushel figure barely moves—one-third of a bushel per gallon is the dependable constant. A bushel of apples weighs 42 pounds and, across most home equipment, yields about 3–4 gallons of juice. Divide 42 by 3.5 and you get roughly 12 pounds per gallon, which is where the 30–40 apple range comes from.

Why the Count Swings

1. Variety and Juice Content

Some apples are far juicier than others. Heirloom and bittersharp cider apples—think Winesap, Kingston Black, or Harrison—can press out close to 4 gallons per bushel. Dry, starchy late-season dessert apples may only give 3 gallons per bushel. The sweeter and more water-rich the apple, the fewer pounds you need by volume.

2. Ripeness and Condition

Fully ripe apples are heavier with juice and crush more easily, so they press cleaner. Windfall or overripe fruit is fine for cider (unlike eating fresh) but very soft fruit loses liquid to the pom and can lower your yield. Avoid frost-damaged or moldy apples, which spoil a whole batch.

3. Your Press and Technique

A small tabletop screw press and a commercial hydraulic rack-and-cloth press extract very different amounts. A home press typically recovers 60–70% of the apple's water weight as juice; a commercial belt press can exceed 80%. Grinding the apples finely (to a coarse pulp, not chunks) and pressing in thin layers also raises yield. If you are just starting, plan for the low end and treat anything extra as a bonus.

Quick rule of thumb: 1 bushel (42 lbs) of apples ≈ 3–4 gallons of juice ≈ 3–3.8 gallons of finished cider. So for each gallon of cider you plan to bottle, set aside about 12–15 lbs (one-third bushel) of apples.

Apples to Cider, by the Batch

Most first-timers do not think in bushels—they think in gallons of finished cider. This table converts common batch sizes straight into apples and weight using the 12–15 lb/gallon midpoint.

Batch Goal Juice Needed Apples (weight) Apples (medium count) Bushels
1 gallon ~1.05 gal 12–15 lbs 30–40 0.31
5 gallons ~5.3 gal 60–75 lbs 150–200 ~1.5–1.8
Half-bushel press load ~1.5–2 gal 21 lbs ~52 0.5
Full bushel press load ~3–4 gal 42 lbs ~105 1.0
15-gallon season ~16 gal 190–225 lbs 475–600 ~4.5–5.4

Hard cider loses a little volume during fermentation as yeast and sediment drop out, so always press slightly more juice than the bottled volume you want. The 5-gallon row assumes you press about 5.3 gallons of juice to end up with 5 gallons of finished cider.

From Tree to Gallon: How Many Trees?

If you are growing your own cider apples, the math closes the loop nicely. A single mature standard apple tree yielding 150–200 lbs of fruit can produce 10–20 gallons of cider in a good year, depending on variety and pressing efficiency. That is roughly 500–700 apples per tree turned into liquid. Two or three productive, pollinating-compatible trees will keep most households in cider across the season. One planning note: apple trees are not self-fertile. You need at least two compatible varieties blooming at the same time (or a crabapple nearby) to set a full crop. A lone tree may look healthy yet bear little fruit, which quietly drops your cider supply to near zero.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many apples does it take to make one gallon of cider?

Plan on about 30–40 medium apples per gallon of cider. That works out to roughly one-third of a bushel, or 12–15 pounds of apples. A standard 5-gallon batch therefore needs about 2 bushels (150–175 lbs) of fruit.

How many pounds of apples are in a gallon of cider?

Expect 12–15 pounds of apples per gallon of cider, depending on variety and how much juice they give up under the press. One bushel (42 lbs) of average apples yields about 3–4 gallons of juice, so each gallon costs you roughly one-third of a bushel.

How many apples do I need for a 5-gallon batch of cider?

For 5 gallons, plan on 150–200 medium apples, or about 2 bushels (roughly 84 lbs). If you press 4 gallons of juice per bushel, two bushels give you 8 gallons of juice, which ferments down to about 7.5 gallons of hard cider after losses.

Does the apple variety change how many I need?

Yes, modestly. Heirloom and bittersharp cider apples are juicier and yield about 4 gallons per bushel, so you need slightly fewer by weight. Dry, starchy dessert apples yield closer to 3 gallons per bushel. A good home blend is roughly 40% sweet, 40% tart, and 20% bittersharp or heirloom varieties.

How many apple trees do I need to make cider for a year?

One mature standard apple tree producing 150–200 lbs of fruit can yield 10–20 gallons of cider in a good year. If you want a steady supply—say a 5-gallon batch every couple of months—two to three productive trees of different pollinating varieties will comfortably cover it.

Putting It Together

So, how many apples for a gallon of cider? For planning, commit to 30–40 medium apples—about 12–15 pounds, or one-third of a bushel—per gallon, and scale up from there. The two levers that change the real number are apple juiciness (variety) and press efficiency (technique and equipment). Pick a blend of sweet, tart, and bittersharp fruit, grind it fine, and press in thin layers to land on the high end of the yield range.
