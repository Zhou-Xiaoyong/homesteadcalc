How Much Water Does a Cow Drink Per Day?

Published: October 22, 2026 · 8 min read

A 1,200-pound beef cow drinks 12 to 20 gallons of water a day. A lactating dairy cow drinks 30 to 50. A dry beef cow or a mature bull sits near 10 to 14, and a 600-pound weaned calf gets by on 6 to 9.

Four numbers cover most of the question. What moves them is weight first, air temperature second, then how wet the feed is, then whether the tank can refill while twenty head are drinking at once. Get the last one wrong and the first three stop mattering.

Nebraska Extension puts the base rate at 1 gallon per 100 pounds in cold weather, nearly 2 in the hottest, with lactation roughly doubling the dry-cow figure. That is where the Livestock Water Calculator starts.

The Rule: One Gallon per 100 Pounds a Day

The 2016 Nutrient Requirements of Beef Cattle lists 1 to 1.5 gallons per 100 pounds for dry cows and bulls, 2 gallons for lactating cows. Penn State's grazing guide, drawing on the NRCS publication Watering Systems for Serious Graziers, reaches the same place from field measurements on a 50°F and a 90°F day.

Class of animal 50°F day 90°F day Typical weight
Dry beef cow 8–12 gal 20–30 gal 1,100–1,400 lb
Lactating beef cow 12–20 gal 25–35 gal 1,200–1,400 lb
Lactating dairy cow 20–30 gal 30–40 gal 1,300–1,500 lb
Weaned calf 6–9 gal 10–15 gal 600 lb
Sheep or goat 2–3 gal 3–4 gal 120–200 lb

The gap between those columns is the whole story. Nebraska Extension's spring-calving example puts a cow at 20 to 24 gallons on a 90°F day, plus 5 to 10 for the calf.

Weight and Class Set the Floor

Beef cows

A dry 1,200-pound cow at maintenance needs about 12 gallons. Add milk and the number climbs past 20, because milk is 87 percent water and every gallon of it comes out of the tank. A 900-pound first-calf heifer may drink 9 while her 1,500-pound herdmate drinks 15 from the same trough.

Dairy cows

Penn State puts a lactating dairy cow at 30 to 50 gallons a day, and heat stress can double it in July. A 2016 model cited by the same team lands a 70-pound Holstein near 24 gallons, which is why a string of 100-pound producers blows through 40 a head. Between 60 and 80 percent arrives through the waterer; the rest rides in on the ration.

Calves

Nursing calves take most of their water from milk and still drink. A 600-pound weaned calf needs 6 to 9 gallons on a cool day, 10 to 15 at 90°F. Set the rim low enough for a calf to reach, or grade the ground beside it. Nebraska Extension notes calves watered from ponds gain less than calves on fresh tank water, and a slow-refilling tank the cows drain first leaves the smallest animals dry.

Heat Is the Multiplier Nobody Budgets For

Below about 40°F a cow sits close to her weight-based floor. At a 90°F daily high, intake nearly doubles and not just from sweating: respiration climbs, feed intake drops, and water she would have taken from grass has to come out of a tank.

The failure mode is timing. Cattle do not drink evenly across 24 hours. On a hot day most of the water moves between mid-morning and late afternoon, so a tank that measures fine at 6 a.m. can be dry by 2 p.m. behind a 3-gallon-per-minute float valve. Test the refill rate at midday in July, not at dawn.

Winter runs lower, not colder

Cold weather drops demand to 8 to 12 gallons for a dry 1,200-pound cow, and ice becomes a bigger problem than volume. Snow is not a substitute: melting it costs rumen heat she would spend staying warm. A de-icer on a GFCI circuit keeps water drinkable and holds intake where the ration assumes it will be.

Wet Feed Changes the Arithmetic

Feed moisture swings drinking water by a third or more. Lush spring grass runs near 90 percent moisture, dry hay sits at 10, silage lands between 50 and 60. A cow on May pasture pulls much of her water from forage. The same cow on January hay drinks nearly all of it, which is the week marginal tank systems run short.

High-salt and high-protein rations push the other way. Both raise urine output, and a cow on a salt-limited supplement drinks more, which is how salt limits intake. Size the tank for the ration you feed. The Hay & Forage Calculator turns winter hay into tonnage and barn space, and the water figure follows.

Sizing a Tank the Herd Cannot Drain

Daily gallons set storage. Refill rate decides whether the herd gets them. Penn State recommends at least 30 gallons per minute into a dairy tank, because cows take 30 to 50 percent of their daily water within an hour of milking. Beef herds need less, but peak-hour demand still runs three to five times the hourly average.

Space matters as much as volume. Allow 2 to 4 inches of tank perimeter per cow, room for 20 percent of the group to drink at once, a rim 24 to 32 inches high, and at least two watering points so one boss cow cannot guard the only tank.

Herd Daily need at 90°F Peak-hour draw Tank for a 3-day reserve Refill rate
10 beef cows with calves ~330 gal ~110 gal 1,000 gal 5 gpm
30 head, beef, mixed ages ~890 gal ~300 gal 2,700 gal 10 gpm
50 dry beef cows ~1,250 gal ~420 gal 3,750 gal 12 gpm
80 lactating dairy cows ~2,800 gal ~1,400 gal 8,400 gal 30 gpm

A well is rarely the limit. One gallon per minute is 1,440 gallons a day, so a 5-gpm well delivers 7,200, more than 200 beef cows will drink in a heat wave. The bottleneck is the float valve, the pipe, and the tank volume, in that order.

Water Quality That Quietly Cuts Intake

Cattle refuse water that tastes wrong, and a refusal looks identical to a supply problem. Test the well annually and any pond in a dry summer.

Measure Safe range What happens above it
Total dissolved solids Under 1,000 ppm 1,000–3,000 ppm cuts performance; above 3,000 the taste drops intake; 5,000–7,000 is off limits for pregnant or lactating cows
Nitrate nitrogen Under 100 ppm Blood loses its oxygen-carrying capacity. Feed and water totals add together
Sulfate Under 500 ppm for calves, 1,000 ppm for adults Sulfur toxicity, and polio in cattle on high-concentrate rations
Blue-green algae No visible bloom Toxic. Fence cattle out of stagnant ponds until it clears

Empty and scrub tanks weekly with a weak chlorine solution. Algae in a stagnant tank is the most common reason a herd goes off water in August, and it gets blamed on the float valve.

A Worked Example on 30 Head

Twenty-five lactating beef cows at 1,300 pounds and five dry cows, calves at side, one tank, central Missouri in July.

  • Lactating cows: 25 × 26 gal = 650 gal
  • Dry cows: 5 × 13 gal = 65 gal
  • Calves: 25 × 7 gal = 175 gal
  • Daily total: 890 gal
  • Three-day reserve: 2,670 gal

Two 1,500-gallon tanks cover it, or one 3,000. For refill, 890 gallons over a four-hour morning window is about 3.7 gallons a minute, so a 10-gpm float valve leaves room for the day it hits 100°F and the herd drinks 1,100 instead. Without that margin the tank empties at the worst hour, and the calves at the bottom of the pecking order go without.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a cow drink per day?

A dry 1,200-pound beef cow drinks about 12 gallons a day at moderate temperatures. A lactating beef cow at the same weight runs 20 to 26 gallons, and a lactating dairy cow 30 to 50. On a 90°F day, add roughly 50 percent to those figures, and count another 5 to 10 gallons for the calf at her side.

How much water does a beef cow need in winter?

Less than in summer, roughly 8 to 12 gallons a day for a dry 1,200-pound cow. The risk shifts from volume to ice. A frozen tank cuts intake, and snow is not a substitute because melting it costs the cow body heat she would spend staying warm. A de-icer on a GFCI circuit keeps the water drinkable.

How many cows will a 100-gallon tank water?

About five cow visits at 20 gallons each, so it suits a small herd only when a float valve refills it at 5 gallons a minute or better. Treat a 100-gallon tank as a bowl, not as storage. Ten cows on a 90°F day need 250 to 300 gallons, which means the tank has to refill four or five times.

Do cows drink more water on hay than on pasture?

Yes. Lush pasture is close to 90 percent moisture and dry hay about 10, so a cow grazing May grass takes a large share of her water from forage. Drinking water rises by a third or more when the herd moves onto dry hay for the winter, which is the week most tank systems first run short.

Plan on 20 Gallons a Head

For a mixed beef herd, budget 20 gallons per cow-calf pair per day in summer, 10 in winter, then add a three-day reserve and a float valve rated above your peak draw.

The exception is a dairy string, where 40 gallons a head is the working number and anything under 30 gpm at the tank costs milk in the first hot week of June.
