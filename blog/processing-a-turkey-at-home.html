Processing a Turkey at Home: 4 Hours from Kill to Chill

Published: November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

A 20-pound live tom dresses to about 15 pounds in four hours, and that bird puts 6.8 pounds of cooked meat on the table on Thursday. The four hours run 30 seconds in 145°F water, 20 to 30 minutes of plucking, an hour of cleaning, and 90 minutes in ice water. The two days after it are where home-raised turkeys get ruined.

Most first-timers process on a Saturday because that is when a second pair of hands is home, then find the bird has to sit until Thursday. That is five days, and raw poultry does not get five days.

The Four Hours, Step by Step

Times run from the moment the bird is caught, not from sunrise. Lay everything out first: cone, pot at temperature, plucking table, and the cooler with half its ice in it.

Step Clock The number that matters
Feed withheld Night before 8 to 12 hours, water always available
Catch, hang, bleed 0:00 to 0:05 2 to 3 minutes, 3 to 4 percent of live weight
Scald 0:05 to 0:10 145 to 150°F for 30 to 60 seconds
Pluck 0:10 to 0:40 20 to 30 minutes by hand, under a minute in a drum plucker
Eviscerate 0:40 to 1:05 Keep the giblets and the neck for stock
Weigh and rinse 1:05 to 1:15 Dressed weight lands at 75 percent of live
Chill 1:15 to 2:45 40°F within two hours
Rest Overnight to 48 hours 35 to 38°F, then roast or freeze

Withholding feed is about a clean gut, not tenderness. Twelve hours with water available empties the crop and most of the intestine, which keeps the cavity clean when the knife slips. Pull the feed at dusk and process in the morning.

Bleeding takes two to three minutes and carries off 3 to 4 percent of live weight, 0.7 pounds on a 20-pounder. Hang the bird by both feet, cut the jugular on one side of the throat, and let it finish before the scald. A bird that goes in still bleeding leaves a dark patch in the wing pit.

The Scald Water Is the Whole Skill

145 to 150°F for 30 to 60 seconds, with the bird moved around the pot the entire time. That window is narrow for a reason. At 160°F the skin tightens and starts to cook, and it tears off in strips while you pluck. Below 140°F the feathers stay locked in the follicle, the plucking takes an hour, and most people give up and skin the bird.

Getting eight gallons to temperature takes longer than anyone plans for. Eight gallons raised from 50°F to 150°F is about 6,700 BTU, or 2 kilowatt-hours. A 1,500-watt electric burner outdoors needs close to two hours to deliver that. A 30,000 BTU propane burner does it in 15 to 20 minutes. Light the burner before you walk to the coop, not after the bird is hanging.

Pluck within a minute of the bird leaving the pot. The follicles tighten as the carcass cools, and a bird that sat five minutes takes twice as long. Start on the breast and back, work out to the wings, then singe the pinfeathers with a torch. A 20-pounder carries about 1.2 pounds of feathers, and the last hide in the thigh and wing tips.

Where the Five Pounds Go

A hen dresses at 72 to 75 percent of live weight and a tom at 76 to 78. Buy toms if the target is a dressed weight: the same 20 pounds on the tag gives half a pound more bird.

What comes off Pounds Share of live weight
Blood 0.7 3.5 percent
Feathers and down 1.2 6 percent
Viscera, including giblets 1.6 8 percent
Head, feet, and neck 0.9 4.5 percent
Trim and wash water 0.6 3 percent
Dressed bird 15.0 75 percent

Fifteen pounds goes into the oven and 6.8 reaches the plate, because cooked meat runs 42 to 48 percent of dressed weight. Weigh the bird before the ice bath, not after: a 90-minute soak puts 1 to 2 percent of water back into the skin and throws off the yield math. Giblets and neck add 6 to 8 ounces of meat and a quart of stock.

Ninety Minutes in Ice Water

Chilling is the step that gets skipped, and it has a hard number attached. Extension guidance is 40°F at the center of the bird within two hours of the kill. A 15-pounder leaves the table at 90°F and will not drop below 70°F in still air on a November afternoon, no matter how cold the porch feels.

The rule that works is one pound of ice per pound of bird, with enough water to cover it. Melting absorbs 144 BTU per pound, roughly four times what the same weight of cold water takes up as it warms.

Dressed weight Ice Water to cover Cooler
10 lb 10 lb 3 gallons 30-quart
15 lb 15 lb 4 gallons 48-quart
20 lb 20 lb 5 gallons 60-quart

Change the water once at 45 minutes. Meltwater sitting at 40°F stops pulling heat out of the bird, and the second batch of ice does the last 20 degrees. Ninety minutes in a 48-quart cooler takes a 15-pounder from 90°F to 38°F.

The garage is not a cooler. A November garage sits near 45°F on a mild night and 55°F by afternoon, and neither is under 40. Nor should a warm bird go straight into the refrigerator: it pulls the box to 50°F and takes six hours to recover. Cooler first, then the fridge.

Twenty-Four Hours, and Why They Are Not Optional

Muscles contract for four to six hours after death and stay that way until the glycogen runs out, 12 to 24 hours at refrigerator temperature. Carve a bird four hours off the kill and the breast is tight and the juice runs pink at the bone even at 165°F. That is rigor, not rawness, and no thermometer fixes it.

USDA gives raw poultry one to two days in the refrigerator, so the safe window and the tender window overlap at 24 to 48 hours. That is the entire margin.

Freezing is the other exit, but only after a day of rest. Dry it, bag it, and it holds 12 months at 0°F before quality slips. Freeze it the afternoon it leaves the ice and the breast stays chewy in January. Bags and shelf space are in the freezer space math.

Turkey Week: Feed Monday, Kill Tuesday, Roast Thursday

Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, 2026. Pull the feed Monday night, process Tuesday the 24th, rest 24 to 48 hours, and the bird goes in at 11:00 Thursday for a 4:00 dinner. That schedule satisfies both clocks, which is the only reason to rearrange a workday.

Sunday processing does not work. It leaves a five-day-old raw bird by Wednesday, or it means freezing and thawing a 15-pounder in cold water, which is 30 minutes a pound and 7 1/2 hours of somebody changing water.

Size the flock backward from the table. Twenty pounds live gives 15 dressed and 6.8 cooked, which is 18 plates at six ounces; 24 plates want 26 to 28 pounds live. The per-person figures are in the pounds-per-person guide, and the feed behind them in what a turkey costs to raise.

A 15-pound dressed bird on a 2 percent wet brine is roughly 350 grams of Diamond Crystal kosher salt, or 2 1/2 cups, in 3 gallons of water. Weigh it rather than counting cups: a cup of Diamond Crystal is 140 grams and a cup of table salt is 290, so three scoops can double the brine. Ratios by salt type are in the brine guide. Pat the bird dry first, or the ice bath water dilutes the pail.

What to Have on Hand Before You Catch the Bird

The failure mode is hunting for the plucker while a scalded bird cools on the table. Lay these out the night before:

  • A pot tall enough to submerge the bird. A 30-quart pot takes a 20-pounder with the legs folded; a 5-gallon bucket takes a 15-pound dressed bird and nothing bigger.
  • A thermometer for the water, not the meat. The window is 10 degrees wide, and a hand dipped in the pot cannot tell 140 from 155.
  • Ice, bought the day before. Fifteen pounds for a 15-pound bird, in a cooler already chilled down with a bucket of water.
  • Two food-grade tubs and a hose. One for the bird between steps, one for giblets. Processing makes about 5 gallons of bloody water, so a sloping driveway beats a lawn.

A drum plucker pays for itself at the third bird and leaves cleaner skin than fingers do, but one bird a year does not justify the shelf space.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long after processing can you cook a turkey?

Twenty-four hours at minimum, and 24 to 48 hours is the sweet spot. Muscles stay contracted for four to six hours after death and take 12 to 24 hours at refrigerator temperature to relax, which is why a same-day bird tastes tight. USDA gives raw poultry one to two days refrigerated, so the safe window and the tender window line up at 24 to 48 hours.

What temperature should the scald water be for a turkey?

One hundred forty-five to 150 degrees Fahrenheit for 30 to 60 seconds, with the bird moved around the pot the whole time. Above 160 degrees the skin tightens and cooks, and it tears off in strips while you pluck. Below 140 degrees the feathers stay locked in the follicle and the bird has to be skinned instead.

How much weight does a turkey lose during processing?

About 25 percent of live weight. A 20-pound tom dresses to 15 pounds, a yield of 75 percent. Hens run 72 to 75 percent and toms 76 to 78, so a tom is the better buy for a dressed weight. Of the 5 pounds lost, roughly 0.7 is blood, 1.2 feathers, 1.6 viscera and giblets, and 0.9 head, feet, and neck.

Can you process a turkey a week before Thanksgiving?

Not and hold it raw. Raw poultry keeps one to two days refrigerated, so a bird processed Saturday is out of time by Monday. Either process Tuesday the 24th for a Thursday table, or freeze after the 24-hour rest, which means a 15-pounder needs about 10 hours in cold water to thaw.

The Numbers to Write Down

Four hours from catch to cooler, 145 to 150°F for 30 to 60 seconds, one pound of ice per pound of bird, 40°F within two hours, then 24 to 48 hours at 35 to 38°F. Feed out Monday, process Tuesday, roast Thursday at 11:00.

The exception is a bird past 22 pounds dressed: 60-quart cooler, 40-quart pot, 20 more minutes in the ice, and the roasting schedule shifts with it. A hen under 12 pounds dressed passes rigor in 18 hours and can be roasted a day earlier. Carcass and giblets give 3 to 4 quarts of stock, and the canning math turns the leftovers into 9 pints.

Sources: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service guidance on refrigerator storage of raw poultry (one to two days), the 40-degree Fahrenheit holding limit, and the 165-degree internal temperature for cooked poultry; Penn State Extension, University of Kentucky Extension, and ATTRA poultry processing guidance on scalding at 140 to 150 degrees Fahrenheit for 30 to 60 seconds, on 8 to 12 hours of feed withdrawal with water available, and on chilling to 40 degrees within two hours; University of Maine Extension and National Turkey Federation figures on dressed weight as 72 to 78 percent of live weight and cooked meat as 42 to 48 percent of dressed weight; USDA FSIS freezing guidance for whole turkey at 12 months for best quality.
