Do Chickens Need Heat in Winter? When to Add It (and When Not To)

Published: November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Ten healthy hens in a dry, draft-free coop do not need heat at 0°F. They need 6 square feet of vent above their heads and a roost bar wide enough to cover their feet. Heat becomes the right answer in four cases: bantams, chicks under eight weeks, a sick or molting bird, and two or three birds alone in a big uninsulated building.

A hen runs a body temperature of 105 to 107°F and wears a coat built to trap air against her skin. At 0°F she is not so much fighting the cold as managing it: feathers fluffed, feet tucked, metabolism turned up. What she cannot manage is a coop sealed tight enough to hold her breath.

What Cold a Chicken Actually Handles

Air movement matters more than the thermometer. A still 0°F night in a dry coop is easier on a flock than 15°F with a draft cutting across the roost at shoulder height, because moving air strips the warm layer the feathers are holding.

Coop temp at roost height What it means What to do
40°F and up Warmer than the birds need Open more vent; watch for condensation
20 to 40°F Normal winter range for standard breeds Nothing, if the litter is dry
0 to 20°F Still fine for a dry, still coop Check combs on single-comb birds
Below 0°F Frostbite risk climbs on large combs and wattles Close roost-level drafts, keep upper vents open
Below -20°F Beyond what bantams and small flocks handle well Add spot heat or move birds

Those last two rows assume dry air. Wet air moves the frostbite line up by 20 degrees or more, which is why a 20°F night in a damp coop can cost a rooster his comb points.

Moisture Is the Real Problem

Ten hens put about a quart of water into the coop air every night, through breath and through droppings that run roughly 75% water. Seal the vents in November to keep warmth in and that quart has nowhere to go. It rises, meets the cold underside of the roof, condenses, and drips back onto combs and wattles.

Frostbite is a moisture problem wearing a temperature costume.

The fix costs nothing. One square foot of vent per 10 square feet of floor, all of it above the birds' heads: a 60-square-foot coop wants 6 square feet of opening behind half-inch hardware cloth. Keep it open in January. Eave and gable vents let moist air leave upward without blowing across the roost.

Two habits undo good vents: a waterer left inside overnight, and bedding that never gets topped up. One tipped bucket is a gallon of humidity, and a wet mat releases moisture all night.

The 35°F Number Is Measured Inside

Minnesota Extension's trigger for supplemental heat is 35°F at roost height inside the coop. Read that twice, because most people apply it to the outdoor forecast.

A tight coop holding 15 to 20 standard birds runs 15 to 20°F warmer than outside air on body heat alone. For that coop to fall to 35°F inside, the night has to reach 15 to 20°F outside and stay there. Three hens in a 10 by 12 shed get maybe 10 degrees of lift, so their trigger shows up around 45°F outdoors.

What a Heat Lamp Actually Heats

Each hen gives off about 10 BTU an hour. Ten hens are a 100 BTU space heater, or 29 watts. A 250-watt lamp is 8.6 times the flock's own output, and it still cannot warm a building.

Here is the calculator's math for a 60-square-foot uninsulated coop with 10 hens on a 0°F night, electricity at 15 cents a kilowatt-hour.

Target temp BTU per hour Heater size Monthly cost
40°F 2,134 700 W $33.80
32°F 1,688 500 W $26.70
25°F 1,297 400 W $20.50
32°F, insulated 436 200 W $6.90

Two things stand out. Dropping the target from 40°F to 25°F cuts the bill 40%, and 40°F was never a chicken number. It is a people number, the temperature at which you can work in a light jacket while you clean. Insulation does more: it drops the demand by 74% at the same 32°F target, and R-13 in a 60-square-foot coop runs $150 to $200.

On a -10°F design night the same coop needs 2,693 BTU, or 789 watts. One lamp covers 40% of that from a cone 18 inches wide. The birds underneath are warm. The waterer in the corner still freezes.

Fire Is the Bigger Risk

The NFPA counted 830 barn structure fires a year between 2006 and 2010. Heating equipment caused 25% of them, and heat lamps alone 15%, which works out to roughly 125 fires a year started by a bulb hanging over bedding. Barn fires peak in January.

A coop is close to the worst place for an exposed hot bulb: dry shavings, dust settling on the glass, birds that knock things over, and a box small enough to be fully involved before anyone smells smoke.

If you run one anyway, hang it on a metal chain rather than the cord, put a wire cage around the bulb, keep 18 inches clear of bedding and walls, plug into a dedicated GFCI outlet with no extension cord, and mount a smoke alarm on the wall outside the door. A flat-panel radiant heater removes the exposed element and the open flame risk both.

When Heat Is the Right Call

Bantams and small flocks

A 2-lb bird has far more surface per pound than a 7-lb Plymouth Rock, and two of them cannot keep a 100-square-foot shed above freezing. Heat the bird, not the building.

Chicks under eight weeks

They have no adult feathers and no ability to regulate. Start at 95°F in week one and drop 5°F a week. A brooder plate is the right tool here; a lamp over a cardboard box is how spring coop fires start.

A sick, molting, or very old bird

A hen that is off feed is not thermoregulating well either. A molting bird in December has bare patches and no insulation over them. Both need warmth for a week or two, not for the winter.

Sustained sub-zero

Two or three days below -10°F, with wind, will frostbite combs on even a well-built coop. That is the week to hang a panel, then take it down.

Water is the one place most flocks genuinely need watts. Ten laying hens drink close to a gallon a day in cold weather, and a 25-watt heated base keeps it liquid for about $2.70 a month. That is the best-value appliance in the coop.

The Winter Setup That Needs No Heater

Four changes cover most of it. Add vent area above head height until it equals 1 square foot per 10 of floor. Set roost bars as 2x4s with the wide face up, 12 inches off the floor and out of the vent path, so a hen can settle her breast feathers over her toes. Keep litter six to eight inches deep and dry. Move the waterer out of the sleeping area or empty it at dusk.

Then check feed. Cold raises the energy a hen spends staying warm, and intake climbs roughly 20% between October and January. Run the bag count through the Chicken Feed Calculator before the first hard freeze, not after the second bag runs out. Frequently Asked Questions

Do chickens need a heat lamp in winter?

For healthy standard breeds, no, not above 0°F in a dry, draft-free coop. The exceptions are bantams, chicks under eight weeks, and a sick or molting bird. A lamp also fails at the job: 250 watts covers 40% of what a 60-square-foot coop loses on a 0°F night.

At what temperature do chickens need heat?

35°F at roost height inside the coop, read on a thermometer hung at bird level. A tight coop holding 15 to 20 birds runs 15 to 20°F above outdoor air on body heat, so that trigger usually arrives on a night near 15°F outside.

Will chickens freeze without heat?

Not healthy adults. A hen runs 105 to 107°F under a coat that traps air, and she settles her breast feathers over her feet on the roost. What hurts birds is a wet coat and moving air at roost level, not the number on the wall thermometer.

Does one heat lamp warm a whole coop?

No. A 250-watt lamp puts out 853 BTU an hour. The same 60-square-foot coop needs 2,134 BTU an hour to hold 40°F on a 0°F night. The lamp warms the birds standing under it and leaves the waterer in the corner to freeze.

The Numbers to Carry Outside

35°F at roost height is the trigger. Six square feet of vent for a 60-square-foot coop. Ten BTU an hour per hen, which is 29 watts for a flock of ten. $33.80 a month to hold 40°F in an uninsulated coop, $6.90 if you insulate and settle for 32°F. Most flocks need none of it.

The exception worth acting on: fewer than five birds in a building over 100 square feet, a bantam flock, or a night that stays below -20°F into a second day. There, 200 watts of radiant panel beats a bulb, and either beats a block of ice in the waterer.
