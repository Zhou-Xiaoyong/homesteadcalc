How Long Do Mushroom Logs Keep Producing?

Published: October 31, 2026 · 8 min read

A shiitake log keeps producing for four to six years. An oyster log on soft hardwood gives two to three. The shorthand that holds across most backyard species is one year of production per inch of diameter, so a 4-inch oak log runs about four seasons and an 8-inch log twice that. The first crop arrives six to twelve months after inoculation, which is the part that surprises anyone expecting a garden crop.

Three things set the clock: how much wood the log holds, how aggressive the species is, and how often you force a flush. A log is a fixed pantry of sugars and lignin, and nothing puts back what a flush spends. That is why a heavily forced log dies young, and why the same species on a thicker bolt runs twice as long.

What the One-Inch Rule Actually Measures

Field & Forest Products, the Wisconsin supplier that has sold plug and sawdust spawn to log growers for decades, states the rule plainly: expect a shiitake log to last one year per inch of diameter. They also put the range at four to six years and about twelve crops per log, the same budget counted in fruitings instead of years.

Diameter carries more weight than length. Going from 4 inches across to 8 inches across does not double the wood, it quadruples it, because volume follows the square of the radius. So the rule is conservative on fat bolts and roughly right at the 4 to 6 inches Cornell's cultivation material and NC State Extension recommend, which is about all one person wants to drill 40 to 60 holes into.

Species Decide the Timeline

Same diameter, different clock. Aggressive primary decomposers chew through soft wood and burn out. Slower species on dense hardwood stretch the same log over more seasons.

Species Productive life First crop Best wood
Shiitake 4–6 years, up to 8 6–12 months Oak, sugar maple, ironwood, beech
Oyster (pearl, blue) 2–3 years 3–6 months Poplar, box elder, willow, soft maple
Lion's mane 4–6 years 6–12 months Oak over birch, for the bark
Chestnut, turkey tail 2–3 years 4–8 months Mixed hardwood, eaten fast

Two consequences follow. Oyster on poplar is the fastest mushroom you can grow outdoors and the shortest lived, so plan on cutting fresh logs every spring. Lion's mane on oak lasts about as long as shiitake but fruits less each year, so it takes more logs for the same weight. For oyster volume without cutting wood, a straw block does the same job in eight weeks; the straw growing walkthrough covers that route from chop to fourth flush.

The First Year Is Mostly Waiting

Spawn run on a 4 to 6 inch oak log takes four to nine months in most US climates, and thick logs or a cool spring push it toward a year. The log is finished when white mycelium shows on the cut ends and the bark feels firm.

Clemson's shiitake bulletin is blunt about the payoff curve. Logs are most productive in the second and third harvesting years, so the year you inoculate is close to a write-off. Twelve logs drilled in March may hand you six mushrooms in October and two pounds the following spring.

When you cut matters as much as what you cut. Take trees in dormancy, late fall through early spring, and inoculate within a few weeks. Cornell puts a hard floor on moisture: wood dried below about 29% is on its way to being firewood. A log that sat in the sun for two months is a wasted log.

One Pound Per Log Per Year, and Why That Number Is Real

Clemson gives the conversion rate directly: the best log conversion ever reported is 33% of dry weight over several years, and working growers see 22 to 25%. The guidance that follows is to average a little more than a pound per log per year.

Run it on a 4-foot, 5-inch oak log: about 0.55 cubic feet of wood, and dry oak runs near 37 pounds per cubic foot, so the log holds roughly 20 pounds of dry matter. At 22 to 25% conversion it owes you 4.4 to 5 pounds. Spread over five seasons, that is 0.9 to 1 pound a year, which lands on the extension number.

Measure Typical figure Notes
Per flush 6–12 oz First flush of the season is the largest
Per log per year 1–1.5 lb Peak in years two and three
Per log, lifetime 2–5 lb Scales with diameter and wood density
Log conversion 22–25% of dry weight 33% is the best ever recorded

Soaking Sets the Pace, and the Pace Sets the Lifespan

Shiitake fruits on its own in spring and fall, when rain and temperature swing together. Most growers force it. Submerge a fully colonized log in cold water, 50 to 65°F, for 24 hours, then stand it in shade. Pins show in three to seven days and you pick five to ten days after that. Then the log needs eight weeks to recharge.

Two soaks plus a natural fall crop gives four harvests a year. Field & Forest's arithmetic explains the trade: a log carries about twelve crops, so two a year stretches over six seasons and four a year over three. Forcing doubles the harvest and halves the life. Just do not force hard for three years and then wonder why year four is empty.

Skip the soak in year one. Partly colonized wood takes on water unevenly, and the wet, uncolonized end is where green mold gets its start.

What Ends a Log Early

Most failures are physical. Torn bark lets competing fungi in. A stack in full sun dries past the 29% line. Dead-stacking like firewood blocks the aeration the mycelium needs, which is why crib or lean-to piles are standard. Termites hollow a log from the inside while the outside still looks sound, and a warm soak in stagnant water turns the interior anaerobic in an afternoon.

Age looks different. A spent log fruits less often, and the caps come out smaller while total weight stays close to what it was. That is the mycelium working harder for the same meal. Two seasons with no pins after a proper soak means the log is done.

Keeping the Pipeline Full

Because logs run four to six years and take most of a year to start, one inoculation never gives a steady supply. Virginia Cooperative Extension budgets about $2,000 per 1,000 logs to start, over a four-year life. Clemson's analysis of a 4,000-log operation assumes 1,300 new logs go in every year, one-third of the stand.

The backyard version is twelve to fifteen logs a year. Inoculate one new log for every three that are producing and by year three you have a stand at different ages, with something to pick in both spring and fall.

Winter is the gap. A log yard goes quiet from December to March, which is one reason to pair it with an indoor crop: the Hydroponic System Calculator sizes a reservoir for greens, and the hydroponics guide covers the nutrient side.

When to Retire a Log

Pick it up. A log that has finished is light for its size, and the bark may be slipping. Split one and look at the cross section: sound wood is pale and firm, spent wood is spongy and riddled with brown zone lines.

Retired logs still have a job. Partly decayed hardwood splits easily and burns hot once dry, so it makes kindling. Laid along a bed edge it holds soil for a season.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do shiitake logs keep producing?

Four to six years on a 4 to 6 inch oak log, longer on thicker wood. The working rule is one year of production per inch of diameter, capped near eight years, with about twelve crops in total.

Do mushroom logs produce every year?

Yes, once the log is fully colonized, but year one is usually light. Shiitake logs are most productive in the second and third harvesting years and taper after that, with two to three natural crops a year and up to four if you force flushes.

How many pounds of mushrooms does one log produce?

Roughly a pound per log per year, so 2 to 5 pounds over its life. A single flush runs 6 to 12 ounces, and log conversion efficiency lands at 22 to 25% of the wood's dry weight.

Can I make a mushroom log last longer?

Use a thicker log of dense hardwood, keep it in deep shade, leave the bark intact, and rest it eight weeks between soaks. Forcing more than two flushes a year trades lifespan for volume, since a log carries a fixed number of crops rather than a fixed number of years.

The Four Numbers to Plan Around

One inch of diameter per year of production, six to twelve months to the first crop, about a pound per log per year, and eight weeks of rest between soaks. Build a log yard on those four and the harvest estimate will hold within a few ounces.

The exception is bad wood. Logs cut in spring when the bark slips, or from a tree down a month in July, colonize poorly no matter how good the spawn was. Trembling aspen is the other outlier: Université de Moncton trials got a first crop in a year or less, then only three to four years of production. Fast start, short life, worth knowing before you cut a truckload.
