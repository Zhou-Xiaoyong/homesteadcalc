How Much Fencing for 1 Acre? Perimeter Math and Materials

Published: September 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A perfectly square 1-acre lot needs 835 linear feet of fence. That figure comes from the fact that one acre is 43,560 square feet, so a square acre measures 208.7 feet on a side — and 208.7 × 4 = 834.8 feet of perimeter. In practice, most homesteaders budget 900 to 1,000 linear feet for an acre, because real parcels are rarely square, and you should add roughly 10% for gates, corner bracing, terrain dips, and the occasional miscut.

Shape changes the answer more than most people expect. A circular acre needs only 740 feet — 11% less fence than a square. A long, narrow 66 × 660 foot strip (still exactly one acre) needs 1,452 feet, or 74% more than the square. Step 1: One Acre Is 43,560 Square Feet — But Shape Is What Costs Money

Every fencing estimate starts from area, then converts to perimeter. One acre equals 43,560 square feet, 4,840 square yards, or 160 square rods. Here is how much fence each common one-acre shape requires:

Shape (1 acre = 43,560 sq ft) Dimensions Perimeter vs. Square
Circle (minimum fence) 118.1 ft radius 740 ft −11%
Square 208.7 × 208.7 ft 835 ft —
Nearly square 200 × 217.8 ft 836 ft +0%
3:2 rectangle 165 × 264 ft 858 ft +3%
2:1 rectangle 147.6 × 295.2 ft 886 ft +6%
4:1 rectangle 104.4 × 417.4 ft 1,044 ft +25%
Long strip (10:1) 66 × 660 ft 1,452 ft +74%

The lesson is simple: the more compact your paddock, the less fence you buy. If you are laying out a new pasture or garden enclosure on open ground, squaring it up is the single cheapest design decision you will make. Fencing a 10:1 strip costs nearly twice as much as fencing the same area as a square.

Perimeter for Other Common Parcel Sizes

If your land is not exactly one acre, here are the square-lot perimeters to scale from:

  • ¼ acre: 104.4 ft per side → 417 ft of fence
  • ½ acre: 147.6 ft per side → 590 ft
  • 1 acre: 208.7 ft per side → 835 ft
  • 2 acres: 295.2 ft per side → 1,181 ft
  • 5 acres: 466.7 ft per side → 1,867 ft
  • 10 acres: 660 ft per side → 2,640 ft (exactly half a mile)

Step 2: How Many Posts for 1 Acre?

Post count is perimeter divided by spacing — plus corner and gate hardware. Line post spacing depends on fence type, and this is where most first-time fence builders either overspend or end up with sagging wire.

Fence Type Line Post Spacing Line Posts (835 ft) Corners + Gate Total Posts
Woven wire field fence 8 ft 105 14 ~119
Woven wire, wider spacing 10 ft 84 14 ~98
Barbed wire (4 strand) 12 ft 70 14 ~84
High-tensile (5 strand) 25 ft 34 14 ~48
Electric polywire/tape 30 ft 28 14 ~42

The "corners + gate" column assumes a square acre: four corners built as H-brace assemblies (two uprights plus a horizontal brace rail each — 12 posts total) and one 12 ft gate with two heavy gate posts. Every additional gate adds two posts and one more brace assembly.

Corners are not optional upgrades. Woven wire and high-tensile fence carry enormous tension — several hundred pounds on a long run — and only a properly braced corner assembly transfers that load into the ground. Skipping H-braces means posts that lean within a season.

Step 3: How Many Rolls of Wire for 1 Acre?

Wire is sold in standard roll lengths, so you are really dividing total wire footage by roll length and rounding up — and total wire footage is perimeter multiplied by the number of strands.

Fence Type Strands Total Wire (835 ft) Roll Length Rolls to Buy
Woven wire field fence 1 fabric 835 ft 330 ft 3
Barbed wire 4 3,340 ft 1,320 ft 3
Barbed wire 5 4,175 ft 1,320 ft 4
High-tensile smooth 5 4,175 ft 4,000 ft 2
Electric polywire 3 2,505 ft 1,320 ft 2

Note that woven wire rolls are 330 ft (20 rods) and barbed wire is 1,320 ft (80 rods, or a quarter mile). Because neither divides evenly into 835, you will always have leftover offcuts — which is convenient, because you will need them for gates, patching, and the low spots where your terrain dips.

Step 4: What Does It Cost to Fence 1 Acre?

Material costs below are typical 2026 US retail prices for a DIY build, calculated for 835 linear feet. Labor, if you hire it out, typically adds $2 to $5 per foot on agricultural fence — call it $1,700 to $4,200 extra for a full acre.

Electric polywire (2–3 strand) $0.35–$0.75 $290–$625 Cattle, trained horses, rotational grazing
High-tensile smooth (5 strand) $0.75–$1.50 $625–$1,250 Cattle, sheep, large perimeter
Barbed wire (4 strand) $1.00–$2.00 $835–$1,670 Cattle, property boundary
Woven wire field fence (47") $2.50–$4.00 $2,100–$3,300 Sheep, goats, pigs, poultry, dogs
Vinyl or PVC rail (3–4 rail) $5.00–$9.00 $4,175–$7,515 Horses, visible frontage
Chain link (4 ft) $8.00–$15.00 $6,680–$12,525 Dogs, security, suburban lots

Two variables swing these numbers hard. First, post choice: a 6.5 ft steel T-post runs $6 to $12, while a pressure-treated 4–5 inch wood corner post runs $25 to $45, and those corner posts are the ones you cannot substitute. Second, terrain: rocky or root-filled ground can double your post-setting time, and every low spot that needs filling adds wire.

Matching the Fence to the Animal

Buying the cheapest fence that "should" hold your livestock is the most common and most expensive mistake on a small acreage. Here is what actually works:

Cattle

Four-strand barbed wire or a single high-tensile electric wire at nose height handles adult cattle reliably. Calves need a lower strand at about 18 inches. Cattle respect electric fence once trained and rarely test a properly charged line.

Sheep and Goats

Goats are the reason woven wire exists. Use 47-inch woven wire with 4-inch square openings, or high-tensile with closely spaced electrified wires and an offset hot wire. Goats will climb, push under, and stand on any fence that gives them a foothold. Add a strand of electric at the top and bottom if you keep a determined herd.

Pigs

Two or three strands of electric at 8, 16, and 24 inches stops pigs, which root under rather than climb over. Woven wire alone will be pushed up and walked over within weeks.

Poultry

Chickens need containment, not strength — but raccoons, foxes, and hawks are the real threat. Use poultry netting with a hot wire at the bottom for digging predators and overhead netting or a covered run where aerial predators are active.

Horses

Never use barbed wire around horses; their skin tears easily and they panic when tangled. Use 3–4 rail vinyl, wood board fence, or high-tensile with a visible top rail at 54 inches.

Don't Skip These Three Budget Items

Material lists built from perimeter alone always come up short. Budget for these before you start:

  • Gates and hardware. A single 12 ft farm gate with hinges and a latch costs $150–$400. Most one-acre paddocks want at least two — one for equipment, one for daily access.
  • Corner bracing. Four H-braces means eight corner posts, four horizontal brace rails, and roughly 12 bags of 80 lb concrete to set the corner and gate posts. That is $150–$250 of concrete alone.
  • Tools you don't own. A manual post driver ($50–$90), fence stretcher or come-along ($60–$150), and a good pair of fence pliers ($30–$60). Renting an auger for a day costs $80–$150 and is worth every penny in hard ground.

And a reality check on labor: setting 100+ posts by hand is a multi-weekend job. Expect to set 20 to 30 posts per day in decent soil, less in clay or rock. If you are fencing to protect a garden or contain animals you already own, build the highest-priority side first rather than waiting until you can afford the whole perimeter.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much fencing do I need for 1 acre?

A square acre needs 835 linear feet. Shape matters: a circular acre needs 740 ft, a 2:1 rectangle needs 886 ft, and a 66 × 660 ft strip needs 1,452 ft. Add 10% for gates, corners, and error, which puts a realistic budget at 900–1,000 ft.

How many fence posts do I need for 1 acre?

For woven wire at 8 ft spacing, about 105 line posts, plus roughly 12 posts for four corner H-braces and 2 gate posts — around 120 posts total. Wider 10 ft spacing drops the line-post count to 84.

How many rolls of wire for 1 acre?

Woven wire comes in 330 ft rolls, so 835 ft takes 3 rolls. A 4-strand barbed fence needs 3,340 ft, or 3 rolls of the standard 1,320 ft length. A 5-strand high-tensile fence needs 4,175 ft, or 2 rolls.

How much does it cost to fence 1 acre?

DIY materials run $290–$625 for electric, $625–$1,250 for high-tensile, $835–$1,670 for barbed wire, and $2,100–$3,300 for woven wire field fence. Contractor labor adds roughly $2–$5 per foot.

What is the cheapest way to fence 1 acre?

Electric polywire at $0.35–$0.75 per foot, or $300–$625 total. It is excellent for trained cattle and rotational grazing, but a poor choice for dogs, poultry, or predator control where physical containment matters.
