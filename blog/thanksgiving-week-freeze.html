Freeze Warning Before Thanksgiving: What to Pick Tonight

Published: November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Pick the tomatoes, peppers, beans, cucumbers, and squash before sunset. Leave the kale, collards, carrots, parsnips, cabbage, and Brussels sprouts where they are. A 28°F night ends the first group and improves the second. With Thanksgiving on Thursday, the freeze costs you the salad and nothing else, provided you spend two hours this afternoon instead of forty minutes tomorrow morning.

Most of the wasted work on a night like this is covering the wrong beds. A cover over tomatoes at 26°F buys four degrees and a false sense of having done something. The same cover over lettuce at 30°F saves the crop.

The Forecast Number Is Not the Garden Number

Extension services draw two lines, not one. Frost damage starts at 31 to 33°F and a killing freeze runs from 26 to 31°F, the difference between nipped leaves and dead plants. Where the thermometer sits decides which one you get.

Official lows are read at 5 feet. On a clear, calm night the air at 2 inches, where your bean pods and lettuce heads actually are, runs 2 to 4 degrees colder, because the ground radiates its heat away and the coldest air pools at the surface. A forecast of 30°F is a bean row at 27°F by 6 a.m. Wind above 8 mph mixes the layers, which is why a windy 29°F does less damage than a still one.

So plan against the forecast minus 3, and for duration as well as temperature. The frost date guide has your averages. Two hours at 26°F kills tissue that twenty minutes at the same number leaves alive, and the coldest stretch runs from 4 to 7 a.m.

What Dies at 32 and What Eats It

Every crop has a temperature at which the cells freeze and burst, and the list is short enough to know by heart.

Crop Damage starts at Tonight
Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, basil 32°F Pick all fruit, ripe and green
Cucumbers, summer squash, beans, okra, corn 32 to 33°F Pick what you can use, then pull the vines
Lettuce, chard, beets, broccoli, cauliflower 26 to 28°F Harvest or cover, both work
Potatoes, tops only 28°F Nothing. Tubers sit 6 in down
Winter squash and pumpkins 28°F Cut with 2 in of stem, cure indoors
Cabbage, Brussels sprouts, collards, spinach 20°F Leave. Flavor improves after frost
Kale, leeks, parsley 10 to 15°F Leave. Kale takes 10°F and comes back
Carrots, parsnips, turnips Roots below 20°F, mulched Leave and mulch 6 to 8 in
Sweet potatoes 32°F vines, 50°F soil Dig tonight, cure 10 days
Sage, thyme, rosemary 10 to 20°F Leave. Rosemary is the tender one

Sweet potatoes are the one line on that table where a single night in the low 30s ruins the part you eat. The roots bruise and rot in storage, so they come out before soil falls to 50°F. Everything else in the tender column is fruit you can still pick.

What Four Degrees of Cover Actually Buys

A light row cover, the 1.0-ounce fabric, adds 4 to 6 degrees. The heavier 1.5 to 2.0-ounce blanket adds 6 to 8. A cotton bedsheet adds 3 to 5, which beats nothing on a 31°F night.

Four degrees is not a small margin. It is the distance between a dead tomato row and a live one at 30°F, and it is irrelevant at 24°F. That is the decision rule: cover when the forecast low minus 3 lands between 26 and 32, and pick instead when it lands below 26.

Three details decide whether you get those degrees. The cover reaches the ground and gets weighted, because it traps heat rising out of the soil rather than heat from the plant. It goes down by 4 p.m., before that soil heat leaves. It comes off by mid-morning, since a covered bed runs near 85°F in full sun on a 60°F day.

Skip plastic sheeting unless it is on hoops. Plastic that touches a leaf conducts cold straight to it, and the leaf freezes at the contact point while the air under the sheet stays above 32°F.

How Much of Tonight's Picking Reaches Thursday

Picking is the easy half. Twelve people at Thursday's table need about 2 pounds of green beans, 6 pounds of potatoes, 5 pounds of squash, and 5 pounds of pumpkin for two pies. Here is what a zone 6 or 7 garden still holds on November 23, and where it sits for three days.

Thursday dish, 12 people The meal needs In the garden tonight Where it waits 3 days
Green bean casserole 2 lb Nothing. Dead at 32°F Crisper drawer, 5 to 7 days
Sliced tomatoes 4 lb 10 to 20 lb of green fruit Single layer, 60 to 65°F
Roasted winter squash 5 lb 15 to 25 lb, cured 50 to 55°F, keeps 2 to 3 months
Two pumpkin pies 5 lb 8 to 12 lb of pumpkin Cure 10 days, then 50 to 55°F
Mashed potatoes 6 lb 20 to 30 lb in the ground In the ground, or 38 to 40°F dug
Kale or collards 3 lb 4 to 6 lb standing Leave it. Pick Thursday morning
Sage and thyme for stuffing 1 handful Standing, hardy Leave it, or dry a bunch

Three days is short enough that storage barely matters, except for tomatoes. Greens hold a week in a damp towel in the crisper, and cured squash holds months at 50 to 55°F, but a green tomato picked tonight is not a tomato you can slice on Thursday. For how much of the meal a garden can carry in the first place, the dinner-from-the-garden breakdown puts about 41 percent of an $80 table on five of nine lines.

Green Tomatoes, a Fifth to a Third of the Crop

An indeterminate planting still carries 20 to 30 percent of its fruit green when the first killing frost arrives. Iowa State's guidance is to take what is solid, firm, full size, and blemish free, then hold it dark at 60 to 65°F for 2 to 4 weeks. Mature green fruit ripens. Immature fruit shrivels and never colors, which is why the test is a whitish cast and seeds coated in jelly.

Store them in a single layer with newspaper between, check every few days, and skip the sealed bag, which grows mold before the fruit colors. One ripe tomato in the box speeds the rest along; one rotting one takes the neighbors with it.

Some should not be ripened at all. Fried green tomatoes take 1 1/2 pounds for a side of twelve: 1/4-inch slices, flour, egg, cornmeal, and 2 to 3 minutes a side in a half inch of 350°F oil.

If It Is Already Too Late

Frozen tomatoes on the vine are a change of plan, not a loss. The cell walls break, so they collapse as they thaw and cannot be sliced, but the skins slip off under warm water and the fruit cooks down at the usual 5 pounds per quart of thin sauce.

What is a real loss: cucumbers and summer squash, hollow and watery, and beans, which turn to string. Basil is finished the moment it blackens. Peppers are the pleasant surprise, diced and frozen with no blanching for 6 months of January chili.

Potatoes look dead and are not. Blackened tops at 28°F say nothing about the tubers 6 inches down, and they can sit a week before you dig. Cure them dark at 60 to 65°F for 10 days so the skins set, then store at 38 to 40°F.

Sweet potatoes are the one crop where being late is expensive. A root chilled below 50°F develops hard spots and rots, so a night at 30°F ends the storage season for anything still in the ground. Dig, cure 10 days at 85°F and 85 percent humidity, then hold at 55 to 60°F.

The Rows to Leave Alone

Carrots and parsnips get sweeter after frost, and both keep in the ground under 6 to 8 inches of straw through winter. Digging them now trades a better vegetable for a shorter shelf life. Stake the row before snow covers it.

Brussels sprouts need about 2 pounds for twelve people, which is one stalk, and the stalk holds through 20°F while the sprouts keep improving. Pick from the bottom up. Cabbage at 20°F and kale at 10°F follow the same rule, and collards and spinach go lower still.

One harvest rule for all of them: pick after the leaves thaw, not while they are frozen. A frozen leaf snaps and wilts; the same leaf at 10 a.m., rehydrated in the sun, keeps a week in the crisper.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many degrees does a row cover add?

A light 1.0-ounce fabric adds 4 to 6 degrees, a medium 1.5 to 2.0-ounce fabric adds 6 to 8, and a cotton bedsheet adds 3 to 5. The cover has to reach the ground and be weighted at the edges, because it is trapping heat rising out of the soil rather than heat from the plant. Put it down by 4 p.m. and pull it off by mid-morning, since a covered bed under full sun hits 85°F on a 60°F day.

Can you eat tomatoes that froze on the vine?

Yes, for sauce. Frozen tomatoes collapse when they thaw, so slicing is out, but the skins slip off under warm water and the fruit still cooks down at the same 5 pounds per quart of thin sauce. Use them within a day of thawing or cook and freeze the sauce. Peppers freeze well diced with no blanching; cucumbers, summer squash, and beans do not survive a freeze worth keeping.

Should I dig carrots and parsnips before a freeze?

No. Carrots and parsnips convert starch to sugar after frost and hold in the ground under 6 to 8 inches of straw or shredded leaves. Push the mulch aside and dig what Thursday needs, then put it back. Dig the whole row only if the ground is going to freeze solid for the winter, and mark the row with a stake before snow covers it.

Do potatoes need to come out of the ground after a frost?

Not that night. Potato tops die at 28°F, but the tubers sit 6 inches down and take no damage from one cold night. Dig within a week of the tops going black, let them cure in a dark 60 to 65°F space for 10 days so the skins set, and then store at 38 to 40°F. Sweet potatoes are the exception and have to come out before the soil drops below 50°F.

The Numbers to Write Down

32 pick, 26 cover, 20 leave. Ground level runs 3 degrees under the forecast, light row cover buys 4 to 6, and green tomatoes ripen at 60 to 65°F in 2 to 4 weeks. Potatoes cure 10 days at 60 to 65°F and store at 38 to 40; sweet potatoes cure at 85°F and store at 55 to 60; carrots stay in the ground under 6 to 8 inches of mulch.

The exception worth planning around is a forecast in the low 20s. At 22°F a cover is decoration, the Brussels sprouts are the only thing worth walking out for, and the two hours are better spent picking green tomatoes and digging sweet potatoes. A still 30°F night is a covering night. A windy 26°F night is a picking night. Different evenings, different work.
