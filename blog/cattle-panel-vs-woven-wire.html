Cattle Panels vs Woven Wire: Cost and Use Cases

Published: November 1, 2026 · 9 min read

A 16-foot cattle panel runs $35 to $45, which is $2.19 to $2.81 a foot before you buy a post. A 330-foot roll of 48-inch woven wire covers the same ground for about $0.79 a foot. Panels cost two to four times more per foot of fence line, and they are still the right buy for a pen, a gate lane, or anything you might move next spring.

The dividing line is length. Somewhere near 200 feet the arithmetic flips, and it flips over a fixed cost: woven wire needs braced corners at $60 to $95 each. Eight of them around a 60-foot pen is absurd. Around an acre it is simply the price of a real fence.

Iowa State's 2024 fence budget puts 48-inch woven wire at $2.34 a foot with posts, top wire, and labor. The wire is 63 cents of that.

What You Are Actually Buying

A cattle panel, also sold as a feedlot panel, is a rigid welded grid: 16 feet long, 50 inches tall, 4- to 6-gauge wire, with 4-inch squares or a mesh that opens from 4 by 8 to 6 by 8. It stays square until something bends it.

Woven wire is a knotted mesh in a roll. Every horizontal wire loops around every vertical stay, forming a hinge joint, so the wires slide instead of snapping. A horse leaning on it deforms the mesh and the fence springs back. The same horse folds a welded panel, and that panel never comes back.

Two things drive the split. A roll covers 330 feet in one piece, so on short runs you buy wire you never unroll, and on long runs that waste disappears. Panels get no bulk discount, and each needs its own pair of posts.

Panels skip the brace assembly. A braced corner is three posts, a rail, and a diagonal wire under tension, and it is the part of a wire fence that fails first when built badly. No tension means no brace, which is why a 10-panel pen goes up in an afternoon.

Break-even lands near 220 feet at these prices. Under that, buy panels. Over it, the gap widens with every hundred feet you add.

Where Woven Wire Wins

Long straight runs

A tensioned roll is one continuous barrier with no joints. Panels make a joint every 16 feet, and every joint is a hinge where a pushing animal concentrates force on two T-posts and a twist of tie wire. Cattle work those joints loose over a winter.

Property lines that must not fail

Neighbors, traffic, and liability sit on the perimeter. Woven wire with a barbed top wire and posts every 12 feet is the standard lineup in extension fence budgets, and it repairs with pliers and a staple hammer. A bent panel needs a replacement panel.

Where Cattle Panels Win

Pens, corrals, and hospital pens

A 50-inch panel is a wall. It takes a shoulder from a 1,200-lb animal without a brace behind it, and it needs no room for a stretcher inside a 12-foot pen. For a working pen or a kidding jug, panels are the correct material.

Anything you will move

Pull the posts, stack the panels, set them somewhere else next month. Two of them and four posts make a 32-foot lane in under an hour, and the same pair is a gate when you need one. Woven wire is scrap the day you move the line, because a stretched roll will not restretch to a new shape.

Mesh Size Decides It for Goats and Sheep

A cattle panel is a cattle product. The standard mesh opens from 4 by 8 inches at the bottom to 6 by 8 at the top, which holds a cow's head out and lets a 20-lb kid walk straight through. A horned doe puts her head through a 6-inch square and does not get it back.

Goat panels and sheep panels fix it with a 4 by 4 grid in the same 16-foot format and price band. Buy those, not feedlot panels.

Woven wire has the same trap. A 12-inch-stay field fence is cattle fence, full stop: the openings are a foot wide. A 6-inch stay holds sheep most of the time. A 4 by 4 sheep and goat mesh, 12.5 gauge and 48 inches tall, is the one that works, and it costs $0.10 to $0.15 a foot more. Pay it.

Panels also have line wires every 6 to 8 inches, and a goat climbs them like a ladder. A 4-inch mesh has a vertical stay every 4 inches, so there is no foothold. Either fence still wants a hot wire on top.

The Gap Underneath

A rigid 16-foot panel spans a dip instead of following it. On rolling ground you get 4 to 6 inches of daylight under the low side, an open door for a goat or a determined dog. Woven wire follows grade because you staple as you go.

Two fixes work on panels: drive a post at every low spot and tie down to it, or run a woven apron along the bottom. Both cost the labor you thought you were saving.

What Kills Each Fence

Panels die at the soil line. The zinc is burned off at every weld and missing on every cut end, so rust starts at the intersections and where the bottom wire sits in grass. A panel on gravel gives 15 years. The same panel in pasture is done in 8 to 10.

Woven wire dies from installation, not weather. Three failures cover most: posts too far apart so the mesh sags into a belly an animal walks over, no top wire so stock fold the mesh down, and a light coating class. Class 1 is 0.28 oz of zinc per square foot and lasts 8 to 12 years. Class 3 is 0.80 oz and lasts 20 to 25, for 15 to 20% more money.

A 4-inch mesh with posts at 12 feet, one barbed top wire, and Class 3 coating outlives the mortgage. Anything less is a ten-year fence pretending to be a twenty-year one.

The Combination Most Homesteads Land On

Woven wire for the perimeter, panels for the working space. On five acres that means 4-inch Class 3 mesh outside with one offset hot wire, and eight to ten goat panels making the pen, the lane, and the gate you open twice a day.

The split is not indecision. It puts the material that resists tension where tension lives, and the material that resists impact where animals crowd. Panels are equipment. Wire is a fence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a cattle panel cheaper than woven wire?

Per foot, no: $2.19 to $2.81 against roughly $0.79 for wire in a roll. Under about 200 feet panels still win the total, because woven wire needs four braced corners at $60 to $95 each and you cannot buy half a roll.

Can you use cattle panels for goats?

For adult goats, yes, but buy goat panels with a 4 by 4 grid rather than feedlot panels with 6-inch squares. A kid walks through a 6-inch opening, and a horned doe can wedge her head in one. Both cost about the same.

How long do cattle panels last?

Eight to ten years standing in grass, where the bottom wire stays wet and the welds rust first. On gravel and moved periodically, the same panel gives 15 years. Class 3 woven wire runs 20 to 25.

Do I need a top wire with woven wire?

Yes. One strand of barbed wire or a hot wire along the top keeps stock from leaning over and folding the mesh down, and Iowa State's budget includes it in every woven wire estimate. Without it the fence fails at the top before the mesh wears out.

The Numbers to Carry to the Store

Panels cost about $3.65 a foot on a long run and $3.77 on a short one, because the price barely moves with length. Woven wire runs $2.30 on an acre and $4.10 on 200 feet, because a roll and four braces are fixed costs. Buy panels below 220 feet, wire above it, and keep eight panels on hand either way.

The exception is ground that rolls more than a foot across 16 feet. There, panels leave gaps you will spend a season closing, and a 4-inch Class 3 roll stapled to the grade is the cheaper fence at any length.

If the perimeter question is still open, fencing for 1 acre walks through the post and gate math, and electric fencing for goats covers the hot wire that sits on top of either mesh. A panel also makes a trellis: one 16-foot section wired to T-posts holds six pie pumpkin vines and the 6-pound fruit they carry, which is how two vines cover a Thanksgiving pie order in 9 square feet per plant.
