Making Wine From Garden Grapes: First-Timer Walkthrough

Published: October 30, 2026 · 8 min read

Sixty pounds of grapes makes five gallons of wine, about twenty-five bottles. That is twelve pounds per gallon for vinifera and sixteen to eighteen for Concord and other slip-skin types, which give up less juice per pound. If your vines carry under forty pounds, drop to a one-gallon batch instead of stretching the fruit with water.

First-year winemaking is three measurements and a lot of waiting: sugar before fermentation, acid at crush, and specific gravity until it stops moving. Garden grapes usually land at 15 to 17 Brix, four to six points short of a dry wine. That gap explains why a backyard batch needs sugar and a Napa batch usually does not. Pounds of Grapes per Gallon

The numbers below assume you press rather than juice, and that you keep the free-run and the pressed wine together. Juice yield runs about 0.85 gallon per 10 pounds of red grapes and 0.90 for white, but pounds-per-gallon comes out higher because pomace, seeds, and lees stay behind.

Grape Lb per gallon 5-gallon batch Bottles (750 ml)
Vinifera (Cabernet, Riesling) 12 60 lb 25
Concord, Niagara 16–18 80–90 lb 25
Muscadine 14–16 70–80 lb 25
Supermarket table grapes 13–15 65–75 lb 24–25

Cornell's home winemaking bulletin puts it in bushels instead: two bushels of grapes yield five to seven gallons of finished wine. A bushel of Concord weighs about 48 pounds, so that is 96 pounds for a five-gallon carboy once stems, seeds, and the wine lost to lees are counted. Budget by the pound, not by the bucket.

Which Grapes Actually Make Wine

Any grape will ferment. The question is whether the result tastes like wine or like fruit punch with a headache attached.

Concord and other slip-skin grapes

Concord, Niagara, Catawba, and Muscadine are Vitis labrusca, and they carry methyl anthranilate, the compound behind grape soda. In wine it reads as foxy, and it buries everything else in the glass. Two ways out: ferment cooler at around 65°F and blend no more than 20% labrusca into a neutral base, or lean into the character the way Niagara producers in New York do. Muscadine is a separate species with thick skins and wants 14 to 16 pounds per gallon.

Table grapes from the grocery store

Thompson Seedless and Crimson run 17 to 19 Brix with almost no acid and no tannin. Fermented alone they make a thin, flat drink that oxidizes within weeks. If that is the fruit you have, add a gram of tartaric acid per liter and a cup of strong black tea per gallon for tannin. It will not become Cabernet. It will become drinkable.

Pick at the Right Number, Not the Right Date

Grapes do not ripen on a calendar. Sample 20 berries from different clusters, crush them, and read the juice with a refractometer. Pick when Brix has held steady for a week and the seeds have gone from green to brown. Taste matters as much as the reading: bitter skins and flat juice mean another ten days will help.

Cut clusters in the cool of the morning and get them out of the sun within the hour. Sort out leaves, raisined berries, and anything with rot before crushing. One moldy cluster in a five-gallon batch will show up in the finished wine.

Fix the Sugar Before You Pitch

Target 22 Brix for a dry wine near 12% alcohol. The formula below comes from Missouri State's extension guide to home winemaking.

Pounds of sugar = 0.125 × gallons of must × (target Brix − measured Brix)

A five-gallon batch of 16-Brix juice needs 0.125 × 5 × 6, or 3.75 pounds. Cornell rounds the same correction to half a pound per gallon on a typical 16-Brix Concord crop headed for 21 Brix, which lands within a few ounces of the formula.

Measured Brix Sugar per gallon Sugar for 5 gal Potential ABV
15 0.88 lb 4.4 lb 12%
16 0.75 lb 3.75 lb 12%
18 0.50 lb 2.5 lb 12%
20 0.25 lb 1.25 lb 12%
22 none none 12%

Dissolve the sugar in a quart of warmed must and stir it back in. Never dump it dry into the fermenter. Dry sugar sinks, the yeast at the bottom meets a concentration it cannot handle, and the batch stalls at 1.040 while you blame the packet.

Crush, Sulfite, Wait a Day

A bucket, a carboy, an airlock, and a siphon cover the equipment side; the homemade wine guide covers what each piece does and what a first kit costs.

Crush by hand in a food-grade bucket, or with a clean 2x4, light enough that seeds stay whole. Cracked seeds release bitter tannins that no amount of aging removes.

Add one crushed Campden tablet per gallon, roughly 50 ppm of sulfite, stir, and cover. Wait 24 hours before pitching. That day kills wild yeast and bacteria on the skins without touching your cultured strain, and it is the step beginners skip most often.

For whites and slip-skin reds, add pectic enzyme at half a teaspoon per five gallons at the same time. Without it a Concord batch can stay cloudy for a year. Rehydrate 5 grams of wine yeast in a quarter cup of 104°F water for 15 minutes, then stir it into the must.

Four to Seven Days on the Skins

Red wine ferments with the skins. White juice gets pressed off first and ferments clear. Punch the cap down twice a day with a sanitized spoon, pushing the floating skins back under the liquid. Skins that dry out on top grow mold in 48 hours.

Hold reds at 70 to 75°F and whites at 55 to 65°F. Warm ferments finish faster and pull more color; cool ones keep the fruit aromas that heat drives off.

Press at 1.010, or let it go dry and press after. Ladle the free-run wine into a carboy, then squeeze the skins in a mesh bag by hand. That pressed portion tastes rough now and fine in six months. Fill the carboy to within an inch of the bung, and top up with leftover wine from a smaller jug rather than water.

Three Rackings, Two Sulfite Additions

Rack off the sediment at three weeks, then every two months, until no new lees form. Each racking exposes the wine to air, so add a quarter teaspoon of potassium metabisulfite per five gallons every time. Keep carboys full. An inch of headspace is fine. Three inches is oxidized wine by spring.

Malolactic fermentation is optional. Run it if the wine tastes sharp and the pH sits above 3.4, and skip it for whites you want crisp. It converts malic acid to softer lactic acid and drops total acidity by about a gram per liter.

Bottle When It Stops Dropping Clear

Most garden-grape wine is ready at six to nine months. Shine a flashlight through the carboy: if you can read the beam, it is clear enough. Fill to within a half inch of the cork and leave bottles upright for three days before stacking them.

For sweetness, add potassium sorbate at half a teaspoon per gallon plus a quarter teaspoon of sulfite, wait a week, then sweeten to taste. Sorbate stops the yeast from reproducing. Without it, a sweetened dry wine referments in the bottle and the corks work their way out on their own.

What Usually Goes Wrong

Symptom Usual cause What to do
Stuck at 1.030 Must cooled below 65°F, or nutrient ran out Warm to 75°F, add 1 tsp yeast nutrient, restart with a gram of yeast in a cup of must
Rotten-egg smell Hydrogen sulfide from stressed yeast Splash-rack into a clean carboy, add nutrient, keep the must under 80°F
Vinegar or nail-polish smell Acetobacter, from air exposure Rack, top up, add 50 ppm sulfite; discard the batch if the smell stays
Corks pushing out Refermentation from leftover sugar Chill, uncork, add sorbate and sulfite, rebottle

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pounds of grapes does it take to make a gallon of wine?

About 12 pounds of vinifera, 16 to 18 pounds of Concord or Niagara, and 14 to 16 pounds for Muscadine. A five-gallon batch runs 60 to 90 pounds depending on variety and yields 25 bottles.

Can I make wine from supermarket grapes?

Yes, with corrections. Table grapes hold 17 to 19 Brix with little acid and no tannin, so they need sugar to reach 22 Brix, plus tartaric acid and a tannin source such as strong black tea. Expect a light, drinkable wine rather than anything structured.

How long until homemade grape wine is drinkable?

Six months for a white and nine to twelve for a red. A Concord tastes harsh at three months and rounds out by month eight. Bulk aging in the carboy does more for it than the same time in a bottle.

Should I add water to garden grape must?

Only for high-acid labrusca fruit, and only as sugar syrup. Cornell's home winemaking guide allows up to 30 percent amelioration with a syrup of 2 pounds of sugar per gallon of water. Plain water dilutes flavor and body along with the acid.

Four Numbers and One Exception

A first batch comes down to four numbers: 12 to 18 pounds of fruit per gallon, 22 Brix before you pitch, 70 to 75°F through fermentation, and six months in the carboy. Hit those and the wine will be sound even if it never becomes memorable.

The exception is a wet, cold September. Grapes that never pass 15 Brix make thin wine no matter how much sugar you add, because sugar is not the only thing that ripens. In that year, press early at 18 Brix for a rosé or blend in purchased concentrate, rather than chasing a red the season did not deliver.
