Can Your Solar System Roast a Turkey? The Thanksgiving Oven Load

Published: November 25, 2026 · 8 min read

A 20-pound turkey pulls about 5.4 kilowatt-hours through a 2,500-watt element cycling at half duty for four and a half hours. Preheat, stuffing, rolls, two pies, the coffee, and the dishwasher bring the day to roughly 11 kWh, which is 38 percent of what an average American house uses in twenty-four hours, spent on one meal. In Chicago in late November that is a 7.3-kilowatt array on a clear day, or a 510 amp-hour lead-acid bank if the sky does not cooperate.

Two lines carry the weight. The bird is 5.4 of the 11, and the rest is a stack of small loads nobody counts, adding up because the oven stays hot from ten in the morning until the pies come out. Bake the pies Wednesday night and the day falls to 9.2 kWh, which is worth more than any panel you could add before Thursday.

The Oven Is the Heaviest Load in the House

A standard 30-inch electric range carries a bake element rated 2,400 to 2,800 watts. The nameplate is not what the meter sees: once the cavity is hot the thermostat cuts the element in and out, and across a full bake it is energized roughly half the time.

That duty cycle sets the hourly rate, about 1.1 to 1.25 kWh at 350°F and 1.6 to 1.8 at 425, since a hotter oven loses heat to the kitchen faster. An hour of oven is roughly what a refrigerator uses in a day, and the panel math for that refrigerator is in the fridge sizing guide.

Four and a half hours at 325°F is the USDA time for a 20-pound unstuffed bird, and the figure in this site's backward oven schedule. Multiply by 1.2 and the bird costs 5.4 kWh. Nothing else comes close. The microwave is 1.2 kW for twelve minutes, and an induction burner is 1.8 kW at 60 percent, which is why the mashed potatoes are almost free.

One inverter detail bites on Thanksgiving morning. A 2,500-watt element wants a 3,000-watt pure sine inverter, and the hard moment is the preheat, when the element sits on without cycling. A 2,000-watt inverter trips there and then runs the roast fine, which is a confusing failure to diagnose at 10 a.m.

The November Math, Where Peak Sun Hours Half

Array size is daily energy divided by peak sun hours, derated 0.75 for wiring, heat, and dust. The variable that ruins the holiday is the sun, and late November is the worst month for it. The solar calculator sidebar lists annual averages, 4.0 for Chicago and New York, 6.5 for Phoenix, 3.5 for Seattle, and November runs near half of those across the northern tier.

Those are clear-sky numbers. A gray Thanksgiving cuts production another 20 to 30 percent, which turns the 7.3 kW into roughly 10.

This is where sizing around a normal day breaks. A homestead that runs on 6 kWh has a 4 kW array and feels fine about it. That array covers about 55 percent of Thanksgiving in Chicago.

What Eleven Kilowatt-Hours Means for the Bank

Divide 11 kWh by 48 volts and the raw requirement is 229 amp-hours. Inverter losses at 90 percent push it to 255. Depth of discharge does the rest of the damage.

A flooded lead-acid bank should not go below 50 percent, so 255 amp-hours of usable capacity needs a 510 amp-hour bank, or five strings of 100 amp-hour batteries in parallel, wired four deep for 48 volts: twenty batteries on a wall, for one dinner. Lithium at 80 percent depth needs 320 amp-hours, which is two strings of four, eight batteries.

Neither is a sensible purchase for one holiday. Size the bank for your normal day, then cover the gap with a generator, a propane oven, or a roaster that runs on any circuit in the house.

The roaster deserves the argument. An 18-quart model holds a 20-pounder with the lid on, draws 1,450 watts, and cooks in a sealed cavity that loses far less heat than a five-cubic-foot oven. Call it 3 kWh for the same five hours, and the range stays free for stuffing and rolls.

Propane is the other honest answer: 4.7 gallons in a 20-pound cylinder, an 18,000 BTU burner, and 91,500 BTU per gallon, which is 0.9 gallons for the bird and a fifth of the tank.

Wood only pays if the stove is already lit. Eighteen pounds of split oak, about one two-hundredth of a cord by the firewood calculator, plus five hours of watching the damper. Thanksgiving morning is the wrong day to learn it.

The Day the Kitchen Outdraws the House

Thanksgiving has a shape that fights a solar house. The sun sets near 4:30 across the northern states, dinner lands at 4:00, and the last hour of the roast happens in the dark, on battery, while twelve guests open the refrigerator forty times and the dishwasher runs twice.

The pump is never the problem, and it is worth saying plainly. A half-horse submersible moves ten gallons a minute on about 370 watts, so 150 gallons for the day costs 0.1 kWh. The oven costs fifty times that in one hour.

Picture the morning: the bank reads 68 percent at 9 a.m., the array makes 900 watts through haze, and the bird has to be in by 11:00 to be out at 3:30. Either the generator runs for five hours, or the bird goes into a roaster, or it gets cut into pieces.

Six A.M. Tomorrow: The Decision Tree

If the forecast is gray and the bank is low, decide before the first cup of coffee. Waiting until ten guarantees a 7 p.m. dinner.

Run the generator for the roast window only. A 3,500-watt unit at half load burns close to 0.4 gallons an hour, so five hours is about 2 gallons. Run it from 11:00 to 4:00, then shut it down and let the bank carry the evening.

Move the bird to a roaster. Three kilowatt-hours instead of 5.8, no inverter surge, and the range stays free for the sides that need 350 and 425 at different times.

Cut it up. A spatchcocked or quartered bird cooks in about two hours at 350°F, which is 2.4 kWh and a much smaller inverter hit. Carving is easier and nobody at the table objects.

Cook tonight and reheat. Roast it this evening, carve, chill fast, and reheat the slices in broth to 165°F tomorrow. Forty minutes at 325 is 0.8 kWh against 5.4, and it is the only option that works if the sun never shows.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many kWh does it take to cook a 20-pound turkey?

About 5.8 kilowatt-hours in an electric range: 0.4 for the preheat and 5.4 for four and a half hours at 325 degrees. That assumes a 2,500-watt bake element cycling at roughly half duty, which is what a thermostat does once the cavity is hot. A hotter oven costs more per hour, since the element fires more often to hold 425 degrees than it does to hold 325.

Can a 3 kW solar array run an electric oven?

It can carry the bird and not the day. In Chicago in late November a 3 kW array makes about 4.5 kilowatt-hours on a clear day, and the turkey alone wants 5.4. The full Thanksgiving load near 11 kilowatt-hours needs roughly 7.3 kW of panels at that latitude, with the array facing south and no shade on the roof.

Will a 100Ah 48V battery run an electric oven?

Not for a whole roast. A 100 amp-hour battery at 48 volts holds 4.8 kilowatt-hours, and a lithium bank should not be pulled below 80 percent, which leaves 3.8 usable. The oven draws 1.2 kilowatt-hours an hour at 325 degrees, so the bank is empty in three hours and the bird wants four and a half. Two of those batteries in parallel still leave nothing for the well pump or the fridge.

Is propane cheaper than electricity for a Thanksgiving turkey?

Not on the grid. The bird costs about a dollar of electricity at the 2026 average rate of 18 cents a kilowatt-hour, against roughly 0.9 gallons of propane, which is two to three dollars at bulk prices. Off grid the comparison flips, because the question stops being price and becomes whether the bank can deliver 2,500 watts for five hours in a month when the sun sets at 4:30.

The Numbers to Write Down

A 20-pound bird is 5.4 kWh, the day is 11, and an oven-hour at 350 is 1.2. Eleven kilowatt-hours is 229 amp-hours at 48 volts, or a 510 amp-hour lead-acid bank, and Chicago in November needs 7.3 kW of panels. A roaster drops the bird to 3 kWh, propane takes 0.9 gallons, and cutting it up takes two hours.

The weight of bird that gets you to 20 pounds, and the 45 percent cooked yield behind its 9 pounds of meat, is in the pounds per person math. The exception is a house that heats and cooks on propane already. Then the oven is a rounding error on the tank and the holiday load is the fridge, the well pump, and the lights, which a 2 kWh bank handles without noticing.
