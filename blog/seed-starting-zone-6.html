When to Start Seeds Indoors in Zone 6: Full Schedule

Published: October 21, 2026 · 8 min read

A zone 6 garden with an April 20 last frost starts its first seeds on February 2 and finishes indoors by April 1. Onions and celery lead, peppers and eggplant follow at mid-February, tomatoes at the start of March, cucumbers and squash at the end of March.

Zone 6 runs from southern Ohio to the Hudson Valley, plus most of Missouri and the high desert outside Santa Fe. Last frost moves from about April 10 in 6b to May 10 in 6a, so these dates assume April 20 and shift with your own. The Frost Date Calculator turns a ZIP code into that date.

Most zone 6 seed starting fails early, not late. A tomato sown February 1 is a 14-inch plant with pale lower leaves by April 1, root-bound and stalled, out-yielded by one sown March 5.

Two rows carry the risk. Onions need 11 weeks because they put on an inch a month under lights; wait until March and you plant sets. A six-week cucumber is a vine wrapped around its own tray.

Zone 6 Is Not One Date

Two zone 6 gardens 60 miles apart can be three weeks apart on frost. Cold air drains downhill and pools in valleys, so a plot at the bottom of a slope runs colder than one 40 feet higher. A south-facing wall buys 10 to 14 days at each end of the season; black plastic over a raised bed warms soil 5 to 8°F above the ground beside it.

Your anchor date is a coin flip

Published frost dates are probabilities: the date with a 50 percent chance of a later frost. Planting tender crops then is a wager. Market growers wait 7 to 14 days past the average, which is why their first tomato ripens before a neighbor's.

6a against 6b

Zone 6b clears its last frost between April 1 and April 20; 6a runs April 21 to May 10. If your date lands late, push every row back three weeks and start nothing before Washington's Birthday.

February: The Slow Crops

Onions from seed want 11 weeks

Seed sown February 2 is ready around April 1, when transplants are pencil-thick and carrying four leaves. Order long-day cultivars. Zone 6 sits between 36 and 42 degrees north, and short-day onions bulb at 10 to 12 hours of daylight, which this latitude does not reach until late March. Long-day types start at 14 to 16 hours, in June. A $4 packet holds 250 seeds.

Onion seedlings take frost to 20°F once hardened, so they go out three weeks before frost. Trim tops to 3 inches and set an inch deep. A 10-foot row at 4-inch spacing holds 30 plants and yields about 25 lb.

Peppers and eggplant want bottom heat

Both germinate at 80 to 85°F in 14 to 21 days. At 70°F the same seed can sit a month and rot. Start nine weeks out, pot up once at four weeks, keep nights above 60°F; pepper flowers abort below 55°F.

March: Tomatoes, Brassicas, Basil

Tomatoes at seven weeks

Sow March 2 for a May 5 set-out: a quarter inch deep, soil at 75 to 80°F until the first loop shows, then lights 2 inches above the leaves for 14 to 16 hours. Seven weeks transplants without a check; ten weeks produces a plant that flowers in the tray.

Brassicas go out before the last frost

Broccoli, cabbage, and cauliflower take 6 to 8 weeks, then go to the garden four weeks before last frost, around March 23, at three or four true leaves. Older transplants button, the head forming at 2 inches. They shrug off 25°F once hardened. Row cover for the first month keeps cabbage moth off.

Basil and lettuce

Basil germinates in a week at 70°F and sulks below 50°F; six weeks is enough, then out a week after frost. Lettuce needs five weeks and takes a light frost, so April 6 is safe. Past mid-April, long days push it to bolt before it heads.

Light is where the cheap setup gives out. One 4-foot shop light covers two 1020 trays, nothing more. Two 40-watt LED tubes for 16 hours a day over ten weeks pull about 90 kilowatt-hours, roughly $15 at 17 cents. A south window is no substitute: glass cuts a third of the light.

A heat mat holds soil 10 to 20°F above room air, the gap between a 68°F kitchen and the 80°F peppers want. Pull it at germination; seedlings left on bottom heat grow soft, stretched stems.

Space math before you buy trays: a 72-cell tray holds three tomato varieties and still leaves 30 cells. Ten weeks later those 72 plants need the shelf twice over, since each moves to a 4-inch pot.

Potting Up and Hardening Off

A 72-cell tray holds a seedling about four weeks, then roots circle the cell and top growth stops. Move tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant into 4-inch pots at the fourth true leaf, set deeper than they grew.

Hardening off, day by day: Day 1, one hour in shade. Day 3, three hours of morning sun. Day 5, a full day in half sun. Day 7, full sun all day, back inside at night. Day 10, out overnight once lows hold above 50°F. Skip it and a tray of tomatoes sunburns to white patches in under two hours.

The schedule has slack for one lost week, not for a plant that spent nine weeks in a cell. If April turns cold, hold seedlings at 60°F and 12 hours of light.

The Fall Window Zone 6 Also Gets

First frost lands between October 5 and October 31, and that date sets a second calendar nobody uses. Fall cabbage and broccoli go into trays in late June, 12 to 14 weeks before frost. Kale for October starts late June to early July, lettuce for September in late July. Fall brassicas size up in heat and head in cool air, and cabbage worms taper off after Labor Day.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start tomato seeds in zone 6?

Between February 24 and March 10 for a last frost near April 20, 6 to 8 weeks of indoor growth. March 2 is the safest single date. Earlier starts flower in the tray and yield less.

Can I start seeds indoors in January in zone 6?

Only onions, leeks, shallots, and celery, which need 10 to 12 weeks and grow slowly enough to stay in a tray. January tomatoes run out of light and root space before the garden is ready.

What is the last frost date in zone 6?

Roughly April 1 to April 20 in zone 6b and April 21 to May 10 in zone 6a. Every published date is a 50 percent probability, so tender crops usually wait another 7 to 14 days. Use your ZIP code rather than the zone number, since two zone 6 towns can be three weeks apart.

Do I need a heat mat to start seeds in zone 6?

For peppers and eggplant, yes. They need 80 to 85 degrees Fahrenheit to germinate and can sit 30 days at 70. Tomatoes sprout fine at 75 to 80, and brassicas, lettuce, and onions need no bottom heat. Pull the mat at germination.

Start With the February Tray

Buy onion and celery seed now and sow both February 2. Add peppers two weeks later, tomatoes on March 2, brassicas that same week, and hold cucurbits until the end of March. About $90 of reusable gear covers a zone 6 garden.

The exception is the late-frost corner of 6a, where April 20 is a hope, not an average. There, move every date three weeks later and accept first tomatoes in August.
