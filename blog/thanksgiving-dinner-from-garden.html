How Much of Thanksgiving Dinner Can You Grow?

Published: November 5, 2026 · 8 min read

A 12-person Thanksgiving table runs about $80 at chain-store prices this year. Thirty-eight square feet of garden bed and one mature apple tree cover $33 of that, which is 41 percent. The turkey is the exception. Raised at home, a 16-pound dressed bird costs $79 against $29 in the freezer aisle.

The split is not random. Garden vegetables are cheap to grow because they are mostly water and they come out of ground you already own. The bird, the butter, and the cranberries are the other $47, and a bigger garden does not touch them.

For the yearly version of this question, the Self-Sufficiency Score rates eight categories including food production and preservation. What follows is the narrow one: one meal, twelve people, item by item.

What Twelve People Actually Eat

Start with the shopping list rather than the recipes, because a recipe tells you how much to buy and never tells you what the meal costs.

Total $80 $33 growable

Five of the nine lines can come off your own ground. The four that cannot are the bird, the cranberries, the bread, and the dairy, and together they are $47.

One honest adjustment before the math looks too good. That $33 assumes the beds, the hose, and the fence already exist. A first-year garden runs $300 to $500 once you buy soil, compost, and a decent fence, so year one is a loss and year three is where the grocery bill starts falling.

Thirty-Eight Square Feet and a Tree

Here is the strange part about that $33. The vegetables that carry it are heavy, so they take very little space. Potatoes run 2 to 5 pounds per plant, one butternut vine sets four to six squash, and an established semi-dwarf apple tree drops 75 to 110 pounds in a good year.

Dish What to plant Space Seed or sets Harvest
Potatoes, 6 lb 4 seed potatoes at 18 in 15 sq ft $2 8–12 lb
Butternut squash, 5 lb 1 plant 9 sq ft $1 8–12 lb
Green beans, 2 lb 4 bush plants 5 sq ft $1 2–4 lb
Pie pumpkin, 5 lb 1 plant 9 sq ft $1 4–6 lb
Apples, 4 lb 1 semi-dwarf, year 8 and up 100 sq ft of orchard $0 after planting 75–110 lb
Total 38 sq ft plus a tree $5

That is a bed four feet wide and ten feet long, with two square feet to spare. It grows $33 of groceries for $5 of seed, and most of that $5 is a packet you will still be planting from in three years.

The apple tree is the outlier and the reason old homesteads are worth more than new ones. A semi-dwarf at year eight produces 75 to 110 pounds, which is eighteen to twenty-seven pies, and it costs nothing after planting except a February pruning. Four pounds of apples off a tree you already have is a better return than anything you can plant this fall.

One caution on the potato line. Six pounds sounds small, but four plants is six feet of row, and if you want mashed potatoes in January too, you need 40 plants rather than 4. The Garden Yield Planner converts your own plant count into pounds, which is faster than guessing at the seed rack.

The Bird Costs More at Home

Twenty weeks, 59 pounds of feed, and a bird that has to be moved twice a day. This is the bill for a 21-pound live tom that dresses out near 16 pounds.

Item Basis Cost
Day-old poult Broad Breasted White $9
Feed 59 lb at $0.55, 2.8 lb per lb of gain $32
Bedding and brooder heat 6 weeks under a lamp $10
Processing Custom processor, or your own Saturday $28
Total for 16 lb dressed $79
Same bird, frozen $1.79 per lb in November $29

That is 2.7 times the store price, and one bird a year never closes the gap. Feed conversion explains most of it. A Broad Breasted White turns 2.6 to 2.8 pounds of feed into a pound of gain, which is the best ratio of any land animal. A heritage breed runs 4 to 5, so the same table eats 80 pounds of feed and lands near $100.

What the extra $50 buys is a bird that spent its life on grass and garden trimmings, eight cups of stock from the frame, and enough fat to roast the potatoes in. That can be worth $50. Just do not run the numbers and call the freezer aisle the expensive option.

The real constraint is the calendar. Poults go into the brooder in the first week of July to dress at 20 weeks, and hatcheries open the next Thanksgiving's orders in January. Heritage varieties sell out by late winter most years, which makes the order a winter task, not a fall one.

What a Garden Will Never Grow

Three items on that list stay on the shopping list no matter how good your soil is, and they are worth understanding before you plan next year.

Cranberries grow in flooded bogs, and Wisconsin and Massachusetts grow most of the national crop. Twelve ounces costs $3. There is no garden version of a bog.

Bread is the quiet one. Two loaves for stuffing take 2 pounds of flour, which is about 2.2 pounds of wheat, and a small plot yields 50 to 60 pounds per 1,000 square feet. That is 40 square feet of wheat, plus threshing, winnowing, and a mill. People who grow wheat for bread do it for flavor and for the variety, not to save money. The rolls are the bigger flour bill of the two: three dozen take 10 cups, and that is 53 square feet of wheat.

Butter takes roughly 2.5 gallons of whole milk per pound, and a quart of cream takes a gallon of milk before you skim it. Without a cow, that $11 line stays $11. The Vegetable Storage Calculator will tell you how long your half of the list holds; nothing on the other half holds at all.

What to Do Between Now and the 26th

Three weeks will not change the percentage much. It is enough time to stop losing what you already grew.

  • Check the squash this week. Cured ten days at 80°F, butternut holds four to six months at 50 to 55°F. A soft spot found today is a usable squash on the 26th. Found on the 25th, it is compost.
  • Keep the potatoes in the dark. A week on a kitchen counter in daylight turns them green and bitter, and the green does not peel off clean.
  • Buy the bird now, not on the 24th. Most ovens cap out around a 24-pound turkey. A 20-pound frozen bird needs four to five days in the fridge at 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds, so it starts thawing on November 21. Check first that the freezer has 0.8 cubic feet of clear shelf, which is the space a 20-pound bird takes.
  • Order for next year. Poults in January, seed potatoes in February, and the varieties you actually want are gone by March.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pounds of turkey do I need for 12 people?

A pound per person is the safe number, so 12 to 14 pounds dressed for a normal plate. Go to 16 pounds if you want sandwiches the next day. Stop at 24 pounds, which is about the largest bird that fits and cooks evenly in a standard oven.

Can you raise a turkey in five months?

Yes, from a day-old poult started in early July. Broad Breasted toms reach 20 to 23 pounds live in 18 to 20 weeks, which puts dressing day in the third week of November. Heritage breeds need 26 to 28 weeks, so they go into the brooder in May.

What can I still harvest in November?

Kale, leeks, carrots, parsnips, and Brussels sprouts stand through light frost, and sprouts turn sweeter after two or three frosts. Everything else on a Thanksgiving table is either in storage already, like squash, potatoes, and onions, or it came from the store.

Is it cheaper to grow your own Thanksgiving dinner?

The vegetables are. Thirty-eight square feet and about $5 of seed replace $33 of produce. The turkey is not: $79 raised at home against $29 frozen, mostly because 59 pounds of feed costs $32 and single-bird processing runs $28.

The Number to Write Down

Thirty-eight square feet and one tree is 41 percent of an $80 table, and it comes out of a season you have already finished. Moving that number next year takes three orders: poults in January, seed potatoes in February, and a second squash plant in June. The bird will still cost more than the store's. Nothing else on the list will.

The mashed potatoes are cheaper than that: eight pounds feeds twelve people, and it is six feet of row.
