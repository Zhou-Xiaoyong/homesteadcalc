Power Outage Before Thanksgiving: Is the Turkey Still Safe?

Published: November 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes, if the bird still has ice crystals in the cavity or reads 40°F or colder, and it has not been warmer than that for more than two hours. Those two numbers decide it. Nothing else does, not the color of the skin, not the smell, and not how long the lights were out.

A 20-pound turkey in a full chest freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours with the door shut. A bird thawing in the refrigerator is on a six-hour clock: four hours of residual cold, then two hours above 40°F before it is a loss. Same outage, opposite outcome, and the difference is which box the bird was in when the lights went out.

The Two Numbers That Decide It

None of these are guesses. A closed refrigerator holds food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds for about 48, a half-full one for 24, because the food itself is the thermal mass. Freezer air at 0°F does almost no work. A block of frozen meat does all of it.

Hours without power Freezer, full Freezer, half full Refrigerator
4 Still at 0°F Near 10°F Nearing 40°F, still usable
6 Still solid Surface softening Over the line. Cook it or toss it
12 Hard freeze in the center Thawed at the edges Gone
24 Ice crystals, soft spots Thawed, still under 40°F Gone
48 Thawing, cavity may still hold ice Warm Gone

That table assumes the door stays shut. A chest freezer loses less than an upright, because cold air is heavy and stays in the box instead of pouring out the front. If the freezer is an upright, treat the numbers as 30 percent shorter.

Six hours is the number people miss. A bird thawing in the refrigerator when the power failed does not get 48. At hour four the box is at 40°F, the clock starts, and by hour six the decision is made for you. That is still enough time to move it to a cooler with ice, or into a gas oven.

Surface Thaw Is Not Thaw

A 20-pound bird pulled from a 0°F freezer feels soft on the breast after a day in a 55°F garage while the cavity between the thighs is still at 25°F. Legs thaw first, then the breast. The cavity, walled in by two inches of meat, is last, and that is where the USDA looks for ice crystals.

So do not judge with a hand. Open the cavity, push the probe in against the inside of the back, and read it. Thirty degrees means the bird is fine and can go back into a cold freezer or straight into the oven. Forty-two means the two-hour clock is running on part of it. Fifty-five means it is over.

This is why the turkey usually survives an outage that ruins everything else in the box. Twenty pounds of frozen meat holds roughly 2,300 BTU of cold: about 560 BTU to warm the meat to 40°F and 1,870 to melt the water inside it. A one-pound bag of green beans holds 115. The bird is the anchor, and the vegetables thaw around it.

The Ice Math for a 20-Pound Bird

Melting a pound of ice absorbs 144 BTU, so pulling 2,300 BTU out of a turkey takes about 16 pounds of ice in a perfect cooler. Double that for a real one. Thirty to thirty-five pounds of block ice in a 48-quart cooler holds a 20-pound bird under 40°F for a day, and a block outlasts cubes because it has less surface area.

Where the bird sits Capacity Ice for 24 hours Ice for 48 hours
48-quart cooler 1.5 cu ft 30 lb block ice 60 lb, drained twice
Chest freezer, 7 cu ft About 175 lb of food 10 lb dry ice 20 lb dry ice
Chest freezer, 18 cu ft About 450 lb of food 25 lb dry ice 50 lb dry ice
Dead refrigerator 18 cu ft 20 lb block ice, top shelf Not worth it, use a cooler

The dry ice figures scale from the USDA standard of 50 pounds for a fully stocked 18-cubic-foot freezer held for two days, which works out to about 2.8 pounds per cubic foot. A 7-cubic-foot chest freezer is not a small 18-cubic-foot one, though: it has proportionally more wall and less food, so 20 pounds is generous and 10 will usually carry a day.

Three rules on dry ice. Do not touch it with bare hands. Do not set it against the bird, since it freezes the surface it touches far below 0°F and toughens the meat. And ventilate the room: a pound of dry ice releases a pound of carbon dioxide as it sublimates, and a chest freezer in a closed mudroom is a bad place to concentrate it.

If the Bird Is Cold but the Clock Is Running

Three plans, in order of cost. Pick one inside six hours.

Hold it with ice. Thirty pounds of block ice in a cooler, bird in a pan on top of the ice, lid shut, meltwater drained once a day. This is the plan when the utility says power is back by Tuesday. A bird that is still frozen solid can go in frozen and thaw there slowly, which is what the refrigerator was going to do anyway.

Cook it now. A roasted 20-pounder yields about 9 pounds of cooked meat, which keeps 3 to 4 days in a working refrigerator and indefinitely in a freezer that still holds 0°F. With an electric oven and no power, this needs a gas grill, a propane burner, or a neighbor's kitchen. The roasting schedule is unchanged: 4 1/2 to 5 hours at 325°F.

Can it. Turkey pressure-canned at 10 pounds of pressure for 75 minutes (pints) or 90 (quarts) is shelf stable and needs no electricity afterward, and a 20-pounder gives nine pints. This suits a bird still under 40°F and an outage with no end date. It is the wrong move for a bird that has been warm, since food above 40°F for two hours or more is not canning stock. Jar math is in the canning scaler; the process is in the leftover turkey guide.

Running the Freezer Instead

A 7-cubic-foot chest freezer draws 150 to 180 watts while the compressor runs, and in a 60°F garage that is about a third of the time. Call it 1.4 kilowatt-hours a day. A 100-amp-hour 12-volt lithium battery holds 1.28 kilowatt-hours, about 1.15 usable, so one battery and a 2,000-watt inverter carries the freezer for a day. Size the inverter for the surge, not the load: startup is three times the running draw.

That is the cheapest way to protect a freezer full of garden produce, and a big enough inverter runs the well pump and furnace fan at the same time. The battery bank calculator takes the load list and returns amp-hours and battery count.

If It Is Already Too Late

Above 40°F for two hours or more, the bird is a loss. Cooking does not rescue it, because the bacteria have already produced heat-stable toxins that no roasting temperature touches. Rinsing does not either. This is the one place where being decisive keeps a household from being sick on Friday.

Then replace it. A commodity 20-pounder at $1.49 a pound is $29.80; a fresh one at the meat counter is $59.80. Stores restock through the Tuesday before Thanksgiving because they always sell out. A 12- to 14-pound bird plus a bone-in breast feeds twelve for about the same money. What does not work is a frozen 20-pounder bought Monday: at 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds, it needs five days. The thaw-days math is why the fresh case is the only one worth walking to.

The forgotten cost: a 7-cubic-foot chest freezer holds roughly 175 pounds of food at 25 pounds per cubic foot, and replacing that at retail vegetable prices runs $300 before the freezer itself. That is what a $20 thermometer and a $60 block of ice are insuring, and it is why the freezer gets the generator before the refrigerator does.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long will a turkey stay frozen without power?

About 48 hours in a full freezer and 24 hours in a half-full one, with the door kept shut. A 20-pounder is usually the last thing in the box to thaw because 20 pounds of frozen meat carries far more cold than a bag of green beans does. If the cavity still has ice crystals when the power comes back, the bird is still safe to cook or refreeze.

Can you refreeze a turkey that thawed during a power outage?

Yes, if it still contains ice crystals or reads 40 degrees Fahrenheit or colder. The USDA allows refreezing at that point, though the texture of the meat suffers because ice crystals that formed during the first freeze break cell walls on the way out. If the bird is already soft and there is time before Thursday, cooking it and freezing the cooked meat holds up better than refreezing it raw.

Is the turkey safe if it sat above 40 degrees for four hours?

No. Perishable food that has been above 40 degrees Fahrenheit for two hours or more should be discarded, and cooking it does not fix the problem because the heat does not destroy the toxins bacteria have already produced. Rinsing does not fix it either. The USDA Meat and Poultry Hotline, 1-888-674-6854, will confirm the call on a specific bird.

How much dry ice does a 7-cubic-foot freezer need?

About 20 pounds for two days, scaled from the USDA figure of 50 pounds for a fully stocked 18-cubic-foot freezer. Keep the door shut, wear gloves, do not let the ice touch the food directly, and crack a window where the freezer sits. Dry ice gives off carbon dioxide as it sublimates, roughly 5 to 10 pounds a day in a chest freezer.

The Numbers to Write Down

Fridge, 4 hours. Full freezer, 48. Half-full, 24. Above 40°F for 2 hours, discard. Refreeze only if ice crystals remain. A 20-pound bird needs about 30 pounds of block ice in a cooler or 10 pounds of dry ice a day in a 7-cubic-foot chest freezer.

The exception is a bird still frozen solid two days out. It needs no ice at all: leave it in the dead freezer with the lid shut and it thaws on the schedule it would have followed in the refrigerator, 4 to 5 days at 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds. The other exception is a bird half thawed on Wednesday with no ice in town. Spatchcock it, run it at 450°F for 80 to 90 minutes, carve, and refrigerate the meat in shallow pans. Dinner still happens, just without a whole bird on the platter.
