How Long to Cook a 20-lb Turkey: The Backward Oven Schedule

Published: November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

A 20-pound turkey at 325°F takes 4 1/2 to 5 hours unstuffed, so it goes in the oven at 11:00 for a 4:00 dinner. That is the whole answer for the common case. Everything else on the day is arranged around those four and a half hours. They are also the heaviest electrical load of the year, at about 1.2 kWh an hour, and the Thanksgiving oven load math puts that against an array and a battery bank.

The clock is what people get wrong. A bird pulled at 3:45 and carved at 4:05 is fine. A bird that goes in at 1:00 because the chart was read backward is a 9:00 dinner. So the useful question is not how long, but how long, starting when.

Why 13 Minutes a Pound Fails at Both Ends

Minutes per pound is not a constant. It falls as the bird gets bigger, because heat enters through the skin and a 24-pounder has less surface for each pound of meat than a 10-pounder does. The USDA chart at 325°F shows the effect plainly.

Bird weight Unstuffed Stuffed Minutes per lb
8 to 12 lb 2 3/4 to 3 hr 3 to 3 1/2 hr 17
12 to 14 lb 3 to 3 3/4 hr 3 3/4 to 4 hr 15 to 16
14 to 18 lb 3 3/4 to 4 1/4 hr 4 1/4 to 4 3/4 hr 15
18 to 20 lb 4 1/4 to 4 1/2 hr 4 1/4 to 4 3/4 hr 14
20 to 24 lb 4 1/2 to 5 hr 4 3/4 to 5 1/4 hr 13

Thirteen minutes a pound is right for a 22-pound bird and wrong by nearly an hour for a small one. Ten pounds at 13 minutes is 2 hours 10 minutes; the chart says 2 3/4 to 3 hours. That gap is the difference between a dry breast and a raw thigh, and it is why people who roast a big bird well every year miss badly on a 12-pounder.

Two more adjustments stack on top of the chart. Stuffing the bird adds 15 to 30 minutes, and the USDA still recommends cooking the stuffing in a separate dish, mostly because the cavity has to hit 165°F too. Starting from frozen adds about 50 percent, which turns 4 1/2 hours into 7 for a 20-pounder and means the bird has to be in by 9:00 in the morning. The still-frozen recovery plan has that schedule size by size, along with the overnight water bath that gets a bird back on the normal clock.

Size the bird first. The pounds-per-person math puts a 20-pounder at 12 to 14 people with leftovers, and 20 pounds is the practical ceiling for most home ovens. Past 24 pounds, the pan is bigger than most kitchens own.

The Schedule, Built Backward From 4:00

Pick the serve time first. Everything below hangs off it, and the 11:00 start assumes a thawed 20-pound bird and a single oven.

Time What happens Why that slot
9:00 am Bird out of the refrigerator, unwrapped, patted dry, salted An hour on the counter takes the chill off the cavity and saves 20 to 30 minutes in the oven
10:30 am Oven to 325°F, rack in the lowest position, oven thermometer on the middle rack A full preheat is 20 minutes; the dial is not a measurement
11:00 am Bird on a V-rack in the pan, breast up, into the oven Four and a half hours to 3:30
2:45 pm First temperature check, through the thigh Expect 150 to 155°F. A reading near 130 means the oven is running cool
3:30 pm Out at 165°F in the thigh, onto a board, oven up to 375°F The rest starts the moment it leaves the oven
3:40 pm Rolls in at 375°F, 18 minutes The only free oven window of the afternoon
3:58 pm Rolls out, gravy on the stovetop from the pan drippings Gravy takes 10 minutes and does not need the oven
4:00 pm Carve, 30 minutes after the bird came out The meat has coasted 5 to 10 degrees upward and the juices have settled

The 2:45 check is the one people skip, and the one that saves dinner. A bird reading 130°F with 45 minutes left is not going to make it, and there is still time to turn the oven to 375°F and buy back 20 minutes. Reading it only at 3:30 leaves no options.

Basting does not shorten anything. Every time the door opens the oven drops and the element runs flat out to recover, which is why a roast checked six times takes longer than one checked twice.

The Oven Is the Real Bottleneck

A thawed 20-pounder measures about 15 inches long, 11 wide, and 9 tall. On a rack inside a 16-by-13-inch roasting pan, with handles, it spans roughly 19 of the 24 inches of width inside a 30-inch range. The rack under it is full for four and a half hours. So is the headspace: 9 inches of bird plus an inch of rack leaves about 6 inches above the pan in a typical oven, enough for a sheet of rolls and not enough for a covered casserole.

This is the constraint that decides the menu. Anything that needs 350°F for 45 minutes has to be cooked before Thursday or served cold.

Side Oven Minutes When it fits
Dinner rolls 375°F 18 Rest window, 3:40 to 3:58

Green bean casserole 350°F 25 Bake Wednesday, 20-minute reheat covered
Mashed potatoes None 25 on the stovetop Any time, held in a covered pot or slow cooker

Two pounds of dough is one sheet pan, 24 rolls at 1.3 ounces each. A bigger batch needs a second 18 minutes, which pushes carving to 4:15. The rolls-per-person math is one and a half per guest with three sides, two if rolls are the only bread.

Stuffing inside the bird costs 15 to 30 minutes and makes the timeline less predictable, since the center of the cavity runs behind the thigh by 20 degrees or more. The stuffing quantity math works out to about 3/4 cup per pound of bird, which is 15 cups for a 20-pounder, and that volume does not fit in a 20-pound bird anyway. Bake it in a dish and the schedule holds.

Read the Bird, Not the Clock

Every chart on this page is an estimate. The thermometer is not. Insert an instant-read probe into the thickest part of the thigh, away from the bone, and again into the thickest part of the breast. The thigh should read 165°F and the breast 160 to 165°F when the bird comes out, because the breast will gain 5 to 10 degrees while the bird rests and the thigh has more fat to carry it.

Two instruments are worth checking before Thursday. Drop the instant-read into a glass of ice water: it should read 32°F, and a probe that reads 28 or 36 is off by enough to matter. Then leave a $10 oven thermometer on the middle rack through a full preheat. Oven dials commonly run 25 degrees off, and 25 degrees low is about 10 percent more time, which is 27 extra minutes on a 4 1/2-hour roast.

A wet-brined bird should be patted dry and given a slightly longer roast, since a wet surface steams before it browns, and it cannot go in frozen. The salt ratio is in the brine guide.

Convection ovens run hot. Most manufacturers recommend dropping the set temperature 25°F and shaving 15 to 25 percent off the time, so a 20-pounder goes to 300°F for about 3 3/4 hours. Watch the fan side at 2 hours.

Once carved, the platter has two hours at room temperature. That is the USDA limit for cooked food, and it is shorter than most Thanksgiving tables run. Carve what will be eaten, keep the rest in a covered pan in a 200°F oven or back in the refrigerator, and get the carcass into a pot or the fridge within the same two hours. A 20-pounder yields about 9 pounds of cooked meat, so there will be leftovers whether or not the table empties: canning the extra turns it into nine pints, and the freezer math is 0.04 cubic feet per pound.

The gravy comes out of the same pan, which is the argument for a rack: the bird sits above the drippings instead of steaming in them, and the pan yields 1 1/2 to 2 cups of fat and juice to build from. Per-person quantities are in the gravy guide.

Three Picks, by Oven

By bird size. Cooking 12 to 14 pounds for eight people, distrust the 13-minutes-a-pound figure and start checking at 2 1/2 hours; small birds finish early. Cooking 20 to 24 pounds, put it in at 11:00, check at 2:45, pull at 4 1/2 hours unless the thigh says otherwise.

By equipment: a 16-by-13-inch pan with a V-rack above 16 pounds, since a flat pan steams the underside. A probe thermometer with a cable, left in the thigh for the whole roast, keeps the door shut. If only one thing gets bought this week, buy the probe.

By oven: with 16 inches of interior height, a 20-pounder fits and rolls ride above it. With 14 inches, or a bird over 22 pounds, bake the rolls Wednesday and reheat them in foil for 8 minutes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you cook a 20-pound turkey at 325 degrees?

Four and a half to five hours unstuffed. The USDA chart lists 4 1/2 to 5 hours for the 20- to 24-pound band, and 13 minutes per pound lands in the same place for a 22-pounder. Add 15 to 30 minutes if the bird is stuffed, and about 50 percent if it goes in still frozen, which is 7 hours for 20 pounds.

Should the turkey rest before carving?

Twenty to thirty minutes, uncovered and out of the oven. The internal temperature climbs another 5 to 10 degrees as heat moves from the outer meat inward, and the juices settle back into the fibers instead of running onto the cutting board. A 20-pounder carved immediately loses close to a cup of juice. The rest is also the only window in which the oven is free for rolls at 375 degrees.

Why does my turkey take longer than the chart says?

Two usual causes. The oven thermostat is off, which shows up as a 25-degree error on an inexpensive oven thermometer and adds about 10 percent to the time, or 27 minutes on a 4 1/2-hour roast. The other is a cold bird: a turkey pulled straight from the refrigerator at 38 degrees needs 20 to 30 minutes more than one that sat on the counter for an hour.

Can you roast the turkey and the sides in one oven?

Not at the same time. A 20-pound bird on a rack and a 16-by-13-inch pan spans about 19 of the 24 inches inside a 30-inch range, so the rack below it is full for four and a half hours. Bake the stuffing and casseroles on Wednesday and reheat them at 375 degrees during the 30-minute rest, when the turkey is out and the oven is finally empty.

The Numbers to Write Down

Twenty pounds, 325°F, 4 1/2 to 5 hours, in at 11:00, out at 3:30, carved at 4:00. Thirteen minutes a pound for a bird in the twenties, more like 17 for anything under 12 pounds, plus 15 to 30 minutes for stuffing and 50 percent for frozen.

The exception is a bird over 22 pounds in a 30-inch oven. At that size the pan leaves no room on either side, the rest window is the only chance the sides get, and a second 12-pound bird roasted in January is a better plan than a 26-pounder in November. The other exception is a late start: past noon with a thawed 20-pounder, spatchcock it, run it at 450°F for 80 to 90 minutes, and accept a flatter bird for a dinner that happens on time.
