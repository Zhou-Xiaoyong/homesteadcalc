How Many Pounds of Potatoes for Thanksgiving?

Published: November 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Eight pounds of raw potatoes feeds twelve people on Thanksgiving, and leaves enough for Friday. That is two-thirds of a pound a guest, 16 cups of mash, and two 5-pound bags.

The number you see quoted everywhere is half a pound per person. That is correct for a Tuesday. On the fourth Thursday everybody takes a first scoop the size of a fist, then a second one after the gravy, so the working figure sits near 0.65 of a pound and you round up.

Eighteen days out, the useful part is what that eight pounds turns into: two bags at the store, or six feet of garden row planted in April.

Half a Pound a Guest, Then Add Two

Start with one cup of finished mash per person. A cup weighs about half a pound raw, because a pound of raw potatoes mashes to roughly two cups. Twelve guests take 12 cups, which is 6 pounds, and 6 pounds is the number that runs short.

Add two pounds for second helpings and the container that goes home with your sister. That is 8 pounds.

Count anyone under ten as half a guest. A table with sweet potato casserole on it needs a third less mash, and a table where somebody brings macaroni and cheese needs half.

What a Pound of Potatoes Becomes

Peeling is the step that quietly shrinks the pot. Russets with deep eyes lose close to a fifth of their weight to the peeler and the paring knife. Thin-skinned Yukon Golds lose closer to a tenth, and they can go in unpeeled if you like the texture.

Butter and milk add volume back, about 6 percent, which is why 8 pounds lands about two-thirds up a 6-quart slow cooker. Above 20 cups, use two pots.

Eight Pounds Is Six Feet of Row

Here is where the holiday question turns into a planting question. University of Maine Extension puts garden potatoes at one to two pounds per foot of row, and ten pounds of seed will plant 50 feet at 8-inch spacing or 80 feet at 12 inches.

At 1.5 pounds per foot, 8 pounds of potatoes is 5.3 feet. Plant 6 and you have a small margin. Two dozen potatoes is not a crop. It is a corner of the row you already have.

Seed follows the same arithmetic. Standard seed pieces run two ounces, so a foot of row at 12-inch spacing takes two ounces, and six feet takes 12 ounces. A two-pound bag of certified seed plants about 16 feet, which returns 24 pounds at that row average, or three Thanksgiving pots.

The per-plant numbers look better than that and both are honest. The Garden Yield Planner carries potatoes at 2 to 5 pounds per plant, which is what a single hill does in good soil with steady water. A whole row averages lower, because some hills sit in shade and some get chewed. Plan at 1.5 pounds per foot and you will not be caught short.

Spacing is 12 inches in the row and 30 to 36 between rows, which is what the Plant Spacing Calculator uses. Tighter spacing gives smaller tubers, not more pounds. Seed goes in at 50 degree soil, about two weeks before last frost, and the row gets hilled at six inches.

November Potatoes Are a Storage Crop

The bag you buy this week was dug in September in Idaho or Maine and held at 38 degrees Fahrenheit since. That is the number University of Maine gives for long storage, paired with 95 percent humidity and no light at all, and it is why a bag bought now keeps into February while a garden potato left in a warm kitchen sprouts by New Year.

There is a catch worth knowing if you fry. Below about 45 degrees, potato starch converts to sugar, and sugared potatoes go dark brown in the skillet long before the inside is done. Home fryers should hold potatoes at 45 to 50 degrees instead of 38. Two to three weeks at 65 to 70 degrees reverses most of the sweetening.

So the Vegetable Storage Calculator says 40 to 45 degrees for potatoes and this page says 38. Both are right: 38 is the keep-it-until-March number, 40 to 45 is the fry-it-golden number. Pick based on what you plan to do with the last five pounds.

Grocery spuds are cured and treated with a sprout inhibitor, often chlorpropham, so they make poor seed. Buy certified stock.

Butter and Milk Scale With the Pounds

The Idaho Potato Commission's ratio is the one to memorize: 2 pounds of potatoes, 3/4 cup of hot milk, 2 tablespoons of butter, 1/2 teaspoon of salt. Multiply everything by four for an 8-pound pot and you get 3 cups of milk, 1/2 cup of butter, and 2 teaspoons of salt.

Cut into even 1-inch chunks, start in cold water, and simmer 13 to 15 minutes. Drain, then put the pot back on low heat for a minute to steam off surface water. Wet potatoes make loose mash.

Rice or mash by hand. A food processor ruptures starch cells and turns the pot gluey in four seconds, and no amount of butter fixes it. Butter first, then hot milk, then salt.

Holding Them for Four Hours

A buttered slow cooker on warm holds mash for about four hours with a splash of milk stirred in each hour. Six quarts takes 10 pounds. Anything longer and the edges dry, so make the pot an hour before the bird comes out, not four.

The day-before route works too. Cook and mash plain, cool it fast in shallow containers, and refrigerate within two hours. Reheat covered at 350 degrees Fahrenheit with the butter and milk added at the end, and get the middle to 165 before it goes to the table. The freezer math for the rest of the meal covers where the leftovers go.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pounds of potatoes do I need for 12 people?

Eight pounds of raw potatoes, which is 16 cups of mash and a little over two cups per person. Two 5-pound bags cover it with roughly two pounds to spare. Six pounds is the strict half-pound-per-guest number and it runs out before the gravy boat empties.

How many pounds of potatoes per person for mashed potatoes?

Half a pound of raw potatoes per person at a normal dinner, which mashes to about one cup. At Thanksgiving figure two-thirds of a pound, because the plate is crowded and people come back for a second scoop. Count children under ten as half an adult.

How many potatoes are in a pound?

Three medium potatoes. The USDA puts a medium potato at 5.3 ounces, and the Idaho Potato Commission says three medium potatoes mash to about two cups. So a pound of raw potatoes yields two cups of mash, and eight pounds yields sixteen.

Can I plant potatoes from the grocery store?

It usually disappoints. Fresh-market potatoes are stored with a sprout inhibitor, often chlorpropham, which is exactly what you do not want in a seed piece, and they carry virus loads that cut yield. Certified seed costs $1.50 to $2.50 a pound and one pound plants about eight feet of row.

The Numbers to Write Down

Two-thirds of a pound per guest, plus two pounds. One pound raw makes two cups mashed. One and a half pounds of potatoes per foot of row. Two ounces of seed per foot. Hold them at 38 degrees for keeping, 45 for frying, and add 3 cups of milk and a half cup of butter to every 8 pounds.

Those five numbers cover the pot, the bag, the row, and the leftover container. The exception is a family that treats mash as the main course and the turkey as a garnish. For that table, plant 12 feet and cook 16 pounds.

For the rest of the menu, the garden share of a 12-person dinner works out what your own ground can carry, and the pumpkin math covers dessert. The yield planner is the one to open before the seed order. Sixteen cups of mash also means a lot of gravy, and a 20-pound bird covers half of it.
