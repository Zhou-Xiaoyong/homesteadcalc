Composting Chicken Manure: How Long Before It Is Safe

Published: October 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Composted chicken manure is ready in three to six months in a pile you turn, and about a year in one you never touch. Pathogens die sooner: 15 days above 131°F with five turnings, then 60 to 90 days of curing before the compost is mild enough around seedlings.

Six hens drop 6 to 8 pounds of nitrogen a year. At the 2 to 3 pounds of N per 1,000 square feet a vegetable garden uses, that covers 2,000 to 3,000 square feet.

The same nitrogen is what makes fresh manure risky. Droppings run near 7:1 carbon to nitrogen, twice as hot as horse manure by weight. Alone in a bin they go anaerobic inside a week and vent the surplus as ammonia. Add carbon and the identical material hits 150°F in three days.

The Two Clocks Behind "Safe"

Two things have to happen before that compost belongs on a bed, on different schedules. Pathogens die on heat. The burning from immature compost goes away on time.

The 15-day pathogen clock

The National Organic Program sets the benchmark extension offices quote: a windrow holds 131°F for 15 days and gets turned at least five times inside that window. Forced-air static piles get credit at 3 days. Below 131°F, Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria outlive the compost.

131°F is a floor. A pile that touches 132°F for an afternoon and cools has not done the job. Well-built piles run 140 to 155°F.

The 60-day maturity clock

Finished compost still carries organic acids and free ammonia that damage young roots. Two months of curing, core under 100°F, turned once a month, breaks them down. Compost that smells like forest floor and holds together when squeezed is close. Compost that still smells of manure is not.

Skip curing and the failure shows up ten days after planting: yellow lower leaves, no new growth, root tips that look scorched.

Chicken Manure Is a Green, Not a Brown

Most poultry composting problems start with treating droppings as bedding. Fresh layer manure tests near 1.5 percent nitrogen, 1.0 percent phosphate, and 0.8 percent potash, close to blood meal and well ahead of anything you haul from a barn.

The rule that works is one bucket of droppings to three of loose browns. A 5-gallon bucket holds 25 to 40 pounds, so weight is a poor guide. Three buckets fills most of a 3-foot bin and lands the mix in the 25:1 to 30:1 band.

Brown C:N Parts per 1 part manure Note
Wheat straw 80:1 3 Best structure; chop it or it mats
Fallen leaves 40–80:1 2–3 Run the mower over them first
Pine shavings 300–500:1 3 Slow; screen them out at the end
Shredded cardboard 200–350:1 2 Pull the tape and glossy print
Sawdust 325–500:1 1–2 Only in thin layers; ties up nitrogen
Grass clippings 17:1 0 A green, not a fix for a wet pile

Coop litter is the exception worth knowing. A deep shavings floor forked out in spring already carries a year of droppings, and the mix lands closer to 25:1 because the carbon sat under the birds all along. The Coop Size Calculator gives the litter area for a given flock.

Manure left in an open heap loses much of its nitrogen as ammonia within weeks. Six inches of straw or a tarp over the top holds most of it.

Building a Pile That Actually Hits 150°F

Size and moisture

The pile has to be 3 feet on each side, 27 cubic feet, or it cannot insulate its own core. Four feet is better. A 4 x 4 x 4 bin reads 140°F on a frosty morning; the same material in a 2-foot wire ring sits at 80°F and never finishes.

Moisture runs 50 to 60 percent. Squeeze a handful. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge and leave a damp sheen without dripping.

What goes in, what stays out

Layer it. Six inches of browns, 2 inches of manure, water as you go, repeat until the bin is full. Keep out dead birds, dog and cat waste, meat, grease, and diseased plants. Feathers break down in six months and are nitrogen-rich themselves, so mix them in.

Check the ratio before you water. If the pile has not passed 120°F in five days, the cause is too little nitrogen or too little water, in that order. The Compost Mix Ratio Calculator takes the materials you have on hand and returns the C:N of the blend.

Reading the Thermometer

A 20-inch compost thermometer costs about $25 and is the only tool that tells you what the pile is doing. Read it 12 to 18 inches deep, in the core, never near the wall.

Core reading What it means What to do
Below 90°F Short on nitrogen, water, or mass Add manure, water it, or bulk the pile up
90–110°F Cool stage only; slow going Turn, then check moisture
110–130°F Working, but under the pathogen line Turn and work in more greens
131–150°F Target band Hold 15 days, turn five times
150–160°F Fast, and nitrogen starts leaving Turn when it falls to 140°F
Above 160°F Carbon burning off; fire risk in dry material Turn, add browns, spread the pile wider

The edges never get hot

A pile reading 145°F at the core has a 6-inch shell that never passed 100°F, and that shell is where most weed seeds and a share of the pathogens survive. Turning is not about speed. It moves the outside material into the middle five times, so the 15-day rule covers the batch instead of the middle of it.

Turn when the core falls from 140°F to about 110°F. In July that is every 5 to 7 days, in a cool October 10 to 14. The Compost Temperature Tracker turns the reading and the days since your last turn into a stage and a next action.

Winter: When the Pile Quits

Under 40°F, microbial activity nearly stops. A pile at 150°F in September sits at 60°F in January, and a Zone 6 pile started in October usually restarts in mid-March. Four months becomes seven.

Stockpile instead of composting. Fork the coop out into a covered bin all winter, keep the bedding mixed in so the ammonia stays put, and build the real pile in March. Manure held under cover five months composts as well as fresh manure and loses less nitrogen than a heap open to rain.

Using It Without Burning Plants

Twenty pounds per 100 square feet delivers about 0.3 pounds of actual nitrogen, 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet and the top of the range vegetables use. Spread it a quarter inch deep and work it into the top 6 inches.

Only 10 to 20 percent of that nitrogen is available the first season. Treat compost as a soil builder for next year's crop and plan a second nitrogen source for corn and cabbage.

Salts are the other limit. Poultry litter compost carries more soluble salt than any other manure compost, and an inch a year for five years shows up as stunted beans. Half an inch once a year, rotated with leaf mold, keeps the load down.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I put fresh chicken manure on the garden in fall?

Only on ground that will sit empty. The organic rule for raw manure is 120 days between application and harvest for crops that touch soil, and 90 days for crops that do not. October to May is 210 days, so a fall application ahead of a summer planting works. A November application under winter greens does not.

How long does chicken manure take in a tumbler?

Eight to twelve weeks to something that looks finished, but a 50-gallon drum rarely holds 131°F through the batch. Use it on flowers and shrubs rather than carrots or lettuce.

Does composting kill weed seeds?

Most of them, if the entire batch spends 15 days above 131°F. Seeds in the cool shell survive, and so do seeds that arrive after the pile stops heating.

Can I compost chicken manure with wood shavings?

Yes, and shavings are among the better browns because they do not mat. Keep the layers thin, expect visible shavings at eight weeks, and screen them out at the end.

The Numbers to Work From

Build the pile 4 feet on a side, layer one bucket of droppings to three of browns, keep it damp as a wrung-out sponge, and turn it each time the core falls to 110°F. Five turns over 15 days above 131°F plus eight weeks of curing puts finished compost in your hands in about four months, and 20 pounds per 100 square feet covers the nitrogen a vegetable bed uses. The exception is the static pile. If yours never gets turned, plan on a year, and put this winter's manure into a bin in March instead of adding it to a heap frozen since October.
