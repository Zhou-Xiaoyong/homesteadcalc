Should You Harvest Honey the First Year?

Published: October 1, 2026 · 9 min read

In most of the United States you should not harvest honey from a first-year colony. A new hive spends its first season drawing comb from bare foundation and raising the bees that will carry it through winter, and it needs every pound of nectar it collects to do both. The one exception is a strong colony started early from a nucleus in a long-season climate that fills two brood boxes and a honey super with fully capped frames by late summer. If that happens, a small harvest is safe — but only after the colony has its full winter stores.

Here is what that means in practice: where a first-year colony's honey actually goes, the stores it must keep for winter, the capping test that says honey is ready, and what to do in year one instead. Year One Is a Build Year, Not a Harvest Year

A colony's first season has three jobs and none of them is surplus honey: draw comb, grow the brood nest to a population that can forage and defend, and pack away enough food to survive winter. Every pound of nectar that goes into wax or stores is a pound you do not get to take.

Comb is the cost beginners underestimate most. Bees burn roughly 6 lb of honey for every pound of wax they make, and a two-deep Langstroth brood chamber holds twenty deep frames a package colony must draw from scratch. Foundation gives them something to build on, but the wax itself is made from honey the colony could otherwise store.

Then comes the winter bee problem. Workers hatching in late summer and fall live for months instead of weeks, and they are what carries the colony to spring. Raising that generation takes pollen and a heavy nectar flow right when most colonies are also short of space — and a first-year hive is doing it with a fraction of a mature colony's workforce.

When a First-Year Harvest Is Actually Safe

The deciding factor is not the calendar, it is the state of the hive on the day you inspect it. A first-year colony is safe to harvest from only when it has cleared every box below and still has capped honey to spare.

Check the Hive For Threshold to Harvest If It Falls Short
Brood chamber fill Both deep boxes (or three mediums) drawn and 80% full No harvest — the colony still needs the space
Winter stores already banked Regional minimum reached before you touch a frame Feed 2:1 syrup; harvest nothing
Honey super At least 8 of 10 frames fully capped Leave the super on for winter
Colony population Bees covering most frames in both brood boxes A thin colony cannot spare food or wax
Install date Early April or sooner, from a nuc rather than a package Late starts almost never build enough surplus
Season length Nectar flow running into August or later Short-season climates: wait for year two

Read that table as a gate, not a scorecard: missing any single line means the answer is no. In practice a first-year harvest happens mainly in the South, parts of California, and any climate with a nine-month forage season — and even there it is usually one partial super.

How Much Honey to Leave for Winter

Before any first-year harvest, the colony's winter stores must be banked. Honey is what the cluster burns for heat, and a colony that runs short in February cannot replace it. Leave more, not less, until you have overwintered a hive at least once.

Climate / USDA Zone Honey to Leave Notes
Gulf Coast, southern Florida (zone 9–10) 20–30 lb Short, mild winters; some forage most of the year
Southern US (zone 8–9) 30–40 lb Occasional hard freezes but a fast spring build-up
Mid-Atlantic, Midwest, mid-South (zone 5–7) 50–60 lb Three to four months with little forage
Northern US, Upper Midwest, New England (zone 3–4) 60–90 lb Long cold snaps; cluster may not reach side frames
Far north, high elevation (zone 2–3) 80–100 lb Assume the top of the range and feed to reach it

A first-year colony in a cold climate needs the top of its range, not the bottom: a small cluster may sit on one side of the hive for weeks and never touch honey stored three frames away. Feed heavy 2:1 syrup in fall until the hive feels heavy — heft the back edge from underneath, and adequate stores should feel like lifting a box of books.

Weight beats eyeballing: a full deep frame holds about 6–8 lb of honey and a full medium super 30–40 lb, so a two-deep hive with 60 lb of stores weighs roughly 25–30 lb over empty. If you are within 10 lb of your regional target in October, feed rather than hope.

If You Are Taking Honey: The Capping Test

Honey is ready when the bees say so — with wax. Once nectar is dehydrated below roughly 18 percent moisture, workers cap the cell with a thin white seal. Uncapped honey is still too wet and will ferment, so the rule is simple: do not pull a frame until at least 80 percent of its cells are capped.

Timing by Region

The main harvest window follows the end of the local nectar flow: late June through August in the South and Southwest, late July through early September in the Midwest and Northeast, and August into early September in short-season northern climates. Never harvest during a flow — the bees will cap that honey within days.

Do Not Feed Through a Honey Super

Sugar syrup landing in a super becomes "sugar honey" — legal to eat, but not what anyone wants to sell or gift. Stop spring feeding when the main flow starts, and in fall feed only after the supers are off the hive.

What to Do Instead in Year One

Skipping the harvest does not mean skipping work — year one builds the habits that produce a 60-pound surplus in year two.

  1. Feed to draw comb. Keep 1:1 syrup on a package colony until it has drawn the frames it needs. Stop when the flow starts.
  2. Stay ahead on space. Add the second deep before the first is full, and the honey super once seven or eight of ten frames in it are drawn.
  3. Watch for queen cells. Inspect every seven to ten days in spring. Swarming halves your workforce right before the flow.
  4. Monitor and treat for varroa. Late-summer mite counts predict winter survival far better than honey weight does.
  5. Bank winter stores and stop. Let the bees keep everything they cap in year one, feed to the regional target, and prepare the hive for the cold.

It is also the cheapest way to learn your local flow: note when heavy nectar arrives, when it tapers, and what bloomed between. That calendar, not a national average, tells you when to harvest in year two.

What Changes in Year Two

An overwintered colony starts spring with drawn comb, a laying queen, and a population no package can match. With comb already built, the bees' priority shifts from construction to storage — which is why a second-year hive in a decent location commonly returns 30–60 lb of surplus and a strong one can exceed 100. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you harvest honey from a first-year hive?

Usually no, and never as a routine. A first-year colony is still drawing comb and building the population it needs to survive winter, so its stored honey is working capital rather than surplus. The exception is a strong colony started early from a nuc in a long-season climate that fills two brood boxes plus a honey super with capped frames by late summer. If that happens a small harvest is safe, but only after the colony has its full winter stores.

How much honey should I leave a first-year hive for winter?

It depends on climate. Gulf Coast and zone 10 colonies overwinter on 20 to 30 pounds, the southern United States on 30 to 40 pounds, mid-latitude states on 50 to 60 pounds, and the northern United States on 60 to 90 pounds. Cold-climate hives with small populations need the top of that range because they cannot move freely to fresh frames in a long cold snap.

When do you harvest honey in the first year if you do take some?

Late summer, once the main nectar flow has slowed and at least 80 percent of the cells in each frame are capped with wax. Capping means the bees have dried the honey below roughly 18 percent moisture, which is what keeps it from fermenting. In most of the country that window falls between late July and mid-September.

What happens if you take too much honey from a new hive?

The colony starves, usually in late winter or early spring when its stores run out before forage returns. A first-year colony has a small population, little drawn comb, and no reserve, so a shortfall is much harder for it to recover from than for an established hive. If you realize you over-harvested, feed heavy 2:1 sugar syrup immediately and keep feeding until the hive is heavy again.

Should I feed sugar syrup to a first-year colony?

Yes, and it is the main reason a first-year harvest is rarely worth taking. Feed 1:1 syrup in spring to stimulate comb building and brood rearing, then switch to 2:1 syrup in fall if stored honey falls short of the regional winter target. Stop feeding while honey supers are on the hive so that sugar syrup never ends up in the frames you plan to harvest.

Bottom line: leave year one alone, feed the colony to its winter target, and let it build the comb and population that make year two worth harvesting.
