How Much Cranberry Sauce Per Person for Thanksgiving?

Published: November 17, 2026 · 8 min read

A quarter cup per person, and no more than that. Twelve guests need 3 cups of sauce, which comes from 4 cups of raw berries, 2 cups of sugar, and 1 cup of water. That is the tested batch at the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and it fills three half-pints with a spoonful left for the cook.

Buy two 12-ounce bags anyway. A bag is about 3 cups of berries and cooks down to 2 1/4 cups of sauce, so the second bag costs $3 and covers the sandwiches on Friday.

A Quarter Cup, and Why the Bag Fights You

The catering estimate runs 1/4 cup a guest, four tablespoons. Holiday tables settle between 1/4 and 1/3 cup; above a third, you are cooking for the fridge.

Kids take a tablespoon and leave it; the uncle who builds a sandwich out of every leftover takes half a cup. The two cancel.

The bag is where the arithmetic goes wrong. Cranberries are hollow and mostly water, and they collapse in heat. A 12-ounce bag, about 340 grams and 3 cups, gives 2 1/4 cups of sauce. That 25 percent shrink is why one bag covers nine guests, not twelve.

One Tested Batch, Scaled to Your Table

The published recipe is 4 cups cranberries, 1 cup water, 2 cups sugar, with a listed yield of four half-pints. The source document allows doubling, which covers a 20-person table.

By volume that batch ends up near three cups, so expect three jars and a bowl for the table. By weight it is 1 pound of berries, 14 ounces of sugar, 8 ounces of water. Sugar is 37 percent of the pot, well under the 60 percent a jam runs.

Cook the berries in the water until they split, eight to ten minutes at a hard simmer. Add a half teaspoon of butter if foam climbs the pot, then stir in the sugar and boil exactly three minutes, no longer.

The pot looks thin when you ladle it. Leave it: cranberry pectin sets on cooling, and sauce cooked down at the stove ends up as candy.

What to Buy the Week Before

Fresh berries land in stores from mid-September through November. Wisconsin grows most of the national crop, Massachusetts second, and the harvest ends before the parade. After Thanksgiving the bags disappear until next fall, so the extra bag you buy now is your December supply.

Frozen berries work the same in cooked sauce. Throw them in still frozen and add two or three minutes to the split stage.

Homemade loses on price. Two bags at $3, three cups of sugar at 70 cents a pound, and five lids at 40 cents each come to $8.90 for 4 1/2 cups, which is $1.98 a cup against $1.29 for the can.

What the extra 70 cents buys is control: your sugar, whole berries instead of gel, and jars that work as gifts in December. If none of that matters, buy the can.

Pick through the bag. Firm berries bounce when dropped on the counter; soft or browning ones go to the compost. Do not rinse until you cook, since moisture in the bag shortens its life from weeks to days.

Cranberries are one of the few Thanksgiving items a garden cannot supply; they grow in flooded bogs. The garden-versus-store breakdown for the whole meal puts them in the $47 that no amount of soil improves.

Half-Pints, Half an Inch, Fifteen Minutes

Cranberry sauce is high-acid, pH around 2.5, so a boiling water bath is the right tool and a pressure canner is overkill. If that still feels fuzzy, which foods need which canner settles it.

Fill hot jars with boiling sauce and leave 1/2 inch of headspace, not the 1/4 inch used for jelly. Sauce foams in the bath, and a jar filled to the neck pushes sauce into the water.

Half-pints are the right jar. One is a cup, or four servings; a pint holds eight, more than one dinner, and an opened jar lasts three or four days in the refrigerator.

Start the clock when the water returns to a rolling boil, not when the jars go in. Water must cover the jar tops by an inch. Leave them in the canner five minutes after you cut the heat, then lift them onto a towel and walk away for twelve hours.

Take the rings off before the jars go on the shelf; a rusted ring hides a failed seal until January. Sealed jars hold a year at 50 to 70 degrees.

Whole berry, crushed, and smooth use the same recipe and the same 15 minutes. The difference comes after the berries soften: leave them, mash them, or press them through a food mill. A milled batch sets looser because the skins, where the pectin lives, stay behind.

One thing cannot be canned. Raw cranberry relish, made in a food processor with an orange and no cooking, has no tested process and keeps three or four days in the refrigerator. Serve it fresh; can a cooked batch if you want jars.

Best Picks for Thanksgiving

Three pieces of equipment do the work; the rest is a pot and a spoon you own.

  • Half-pint jars. Eight ounces matches a 4-cup batch, and wide-mouth is easier to fill from a ladle.
  • A boiling water canner with a rack. A 21-quart pot holds nine pints at once, so a doubled batch of half-pints fits in one load.
  • A food mill. Only for smooth sauce; a sieve and a spatula do the same job for less money.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bags of cranberries do I need for 12 people?

Two bags, and you will freeze about two cups of berries. Twelve people at a quarter cup each need 3 cups of sauce, which takes 4 cups of raw berries, 2 cups of sugar, and 1 cup of water. A 12-ounce bag holds about 3 cups, so one and a third bags make the batch and the second bag covers the holiday leftovers.

Does homemade cranberry sauce need added pectin?

No. Cranberries carry enough pectin in their skins to set on their own, and the gel forms as the sauce cools, not while it boils. That is why a pot that looks like soup at the three-minute mark is usually fine the next morning, and why a batch pressed through a sieve comes out looser than a whole-berry batch: the skins stayed behind in the mill.

Can I make cranberry sauce ahead of time and freeze it?

Yes, and it freezes better than it refrigerates. Sauce keeps three to four days in the refrigerator, up to three months in a freezer container, and a year in sealed half-pint jars. Make it the weekend before Thanksgiving, thaw a container overnight in the refrigerator, and stir it before it goes on the table because it will weep a little.

Can you water bath can cranberry sauce with less sugar?

The acid in the berries is what makes this safe, not the sugar, so a lighter batch will not poison anyone. The catch is that no published process time exists for a reduced-sugar version, and the tested ratio of 4 cups berries to 2 cups sugar is the only one with a 15-minute schedule behind it. Keep the tested ratio for jars headed to the pantry and cut sugar only in sauce you will eat within a week.

The Numbers to Write Down

Three cups of sauce for twelve people, from 4 cups of berries, 2 cups of sugar, and 1 cup of water. Two bags on the counter, three half-pints in the canner, 15 minutes once the water boils again.

Everything else on the table has its own arithmetic. The pounds of turkey per person decide the bird, the gravy count runs a third of a cup a guest, and the stuffing batch is a separate pan. The sauce is the cheap line and the one people notice when it runs out.

Sauce on the outside of a sealed jar is not a failure; wipe it cold and check the seal in the morning. The rest goes on Friday's sandwich, which is why the leftover turkey gets canned in the first place.
