How Much Gravy for 12 People?

Published: November 11, 2026 · 8 min read

Five cups of gravy feeds twelve people at Thanksgiving. A third of a cup a guest, plus one cup in reserve, thickened with 10 tablespoons of flour and 10 of turkey fat.

The bird will not hand you five cups. A 20-pound turkey leaves 3 to 4 cups in the roasting pan and about a third of that is clear fat. Skim it and you are holding 2 to 2 1/2 cups of juice, so three cups of the gravy have to come from somewhere else.

Fifteen days out, that is the useful part. Build those three cups now and Thursday is a deglazing job instead of a scramble.

A Third of a Cup a Guest

Holiday gravy runs a third of a cup a person, and catering math lands in the same place: 100 guests, 33 cups. It assumes gravy is one of several wet things on the plate.

Set mashed potatoes and stuffing side by side and it climbs toward half a cup. Both are sponges, and the second pass through the line is where the boat empties. Sixteen cups of mash for twelve people is a lot of surface area asking for gravy.

Round up, always. Gravy is the first thing to run out and the cheapest to have extra of, and Friday's sandwich is dry without it.

What a Twenty-Pound Bird Gives Up

Drippings scale with the bird, slowly. A 12-pounder leaves 1 1/2 to 2 cups in the pan, a 20-pounder 3 to 4, and past 24 pounds the pan is the limit rather than the meat.

Volume is not usable liquid. Warm drippings settle into two layers in a measuring cup within five minutes, and the yellow fat on top runs close to a third of the total, leaving 2 to 2 1/2 cups of juice from a bird that fed twelve.

Two things shrink it further. A stuffed bird holds juices in the cavity instead of the pan, so a stuffed turkey runs dry at the bottom. A brined bird weeps salt water for the first hour, adding volume and diluting flavor at once. If you put that salt there yourself, the brine arithmetic starts at 430 grams for a 20-pounder.

Pour the drippings into a separator or a quart jar, leave the browned bits in the pan, set it across two burners on medium, pour in a cup of stock and scrape for a minute. Those bits are why pan gravy tastes like roasted meat and stock gravy does not.

The Roux Is Two Numbers

Two tablespoons of fat and two of flour per cup of liquid make gravy that coats the back of a spoon. One and a half of each gives something pourable that will not set on the plate. Pick one and stay with it.

A tablespoon of all-purpose flour weighs about 8 grams, so 10 tablespoons is 80 grams, a bit over half a cup by volume. That is where the old shorthand comes from: half a cup of fat, half a cup of flour, four cups of liquid, the same ratio in rounder units.

Order of operations

Fat into a dry pan over medium. Flour in, whisk, two minutes until it smells like toast rather than paste. Then hot liquid in three pours, whisking flat between each one. Cold liquid hitting a hot roux seizes, and whisking does not bring it back evenly.

Simmer 5 to 10 minutes uncovered. Gravy thickens as it stands, so pull it off the heat slightly looser than the target.

Cornstarch, and why it fails here

Cornstarch thickens at half the volume, one tablespoon per cup, and gives a glossy, nearly clear gravy. It goes thin after a hard reheat and grainy after freezing. For a batch eaten Thursday, fine. With leftovers in mind, use flour.

Where the Missing Cups Come From

Twelve guests need five cups of liquid. The pan supplies 2 to 2 1/2. The rest is a sourcing problem with four answers.

The giblet pot costs nothing and runs in the oven's shadow. Neck, heart, and gizzard into a saucepan, covered with water by an inch, gentle simmer for 90 minutes. Leave the liver out; it turns the broth bitter within twenty minutes.

Wings are for volume. Two pounds, roasted at 400 degrees Fahrenheit for 45 minutes so the skin browns, then covered with water and simmered two hours. Strain, chill, lift the fat cap, and you have four cups of stock for five or six dollars. Do it this week and freeze it in two-cup portions.

Four Quarts Nobody Planned For

Friday morning the carcass is a stock problem. A 20-pound frame broken to fit a 12-quart pot, covered with water by an inch, gives 4 to 6 quarts. That is more gravy than anyone needs and twice what a freezer bag will take.

National Center for Home Food Preservation's tested procedure simmers a picked poultry carcass only 30 to 45 minutes before jarring, not the four hours most soup recipes ask for. The canning does the rest, and a longer simmer only concentrates what is already there, salt included.

Then it goes in jars. Hot pack, 1 inch of headspace, pints 20 minutes and quarts 25 at 11 pounds on a dial gauge to 2,000 feet, 10 on a weighted gauge to 1,000, stepping to 12 pounds by 4,000 feet. Never a water bath.

Salt, Heat, and the Two-Hour Rule

Do not salt gravy until it is finished. Drippings from a brined or self-basting bird are already at the edge of salty, and reducing concentrates whatever is in the pot. Taste on a piece of bread rather than a spoon; a spoon always reads hotter and saltier than the plate does.

Hold finished gravy at 140 degrees Fahrenheit or above, which means a low burner or a preheated slow cooker, not a warm corner of the stove. Leftovers go in shallow containers within two hours, keep 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator, and get reheated to a rolling boil before they are served again. Gravy made with cream does not freeze well and should not be kept past the fourth day.

What Five Cups Costs

Eighty grams of flour is a nickel's worth and the fat is a byproduct. Three cups of boxed stock at $2.50 to $3.50 a quart puts five cups of homemade gravy near $3.

Jarred gravy runs $2.50 to $3 for 12 ounces. Five cups is 40 ounces, so the same volume is three and a third jars, $8 to $10, and it tastes like the jar. The gap widens once the stock is yours: wings at $5 to $7 make four cups, and a carcass makes twenty-four.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much gravy per person for Thanksgiving?

A third of a cup a guest when gravy is one of several sauces, half a cup when mashed potatoes and stuffing are both on the plate. Twelve people at the third-of-a-cup rate is 4 cups, and five is the number to actually make.

How much gravy do the drippings from a 20-pound turkey make?

Three to four cups in the pan, about a third of it clear fat. Skim the fat and you are left with 2 to 2 1/2 cups of juice, which is roughly half of what twelve guests need. The fat is never the shortage; the liquid is.

Can I make gravy ahead of time?

Yes, the base. Make a roux with stock two or three days ahead, cool it, and refrigerate it in a jar. On Thursday you whisk in the hot drippings and reheat. Leave the salt out of the base, because the drippings are already seasoned.

Can you pressure can turkey gravy?

No. The USDA canning guide has no tested process for flour-thickened gravy, and a thickener slows heat penetration into the jar. Can the stock plain instead, pints 20 minutes and quarts 25, then thicken it when you open the jar.

The Numbers to Write Down

A third of a cup a guest, five cups for twelve, 2 tablespoons each of fat and flour per cup, 2 to 2 1/2 cups of juice off a 20-pound bird, 20 and 25 minutes at 11 pounds, 4 to 6 quarts off the carcass. Buy two pounds of wings this week.

The exception is a table that puts gravy on everything, bread included. Half a cup a guest, eight cups, and enough that nobody rations the last of the boat. The freezer plan has room for the stock bags either way.
