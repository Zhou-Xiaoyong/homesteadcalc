Startup Costs for Beekeeping: A Realistic Budget

Published: October 27, 2026 · 9 min read

One hive, all new, bees included, runs $600 to $1,100. About $900 is the honest middle. Two hives land near $1,200 to $1,400, not double, because the suit, the smoker, and the hive tool serve both. The range comes from three decisions: assembled or unassembled woodenware, package or nucleus, and whether you own an extractor in year one.

None of that money returns honey in the first season. A new colony spends the summer drawing comb and raising the bees that must survive winter, so a first-year hive gives you 0 to 20 pounds. At $900 spent, that is $90 a pound, the most expensive honey you will ever own.

Prices are 2026 retail from the three suppliers most beginners order from. Bees are the line that moves: packages are priced in January and good apiaries sell out by March.

Where the Money Actually Goes

The hive body and the bees together are more than half the bill before you own a veil. Everything after that is small, and that is what makes the $600 version possible.

The lean column adds to $592, the typical path without an extractor to about $930, and a $170 extractor pushes it to $1,100. That explains most of the gap between keepers who say this costs $500 and the ones who say $1,500.

Two Hives for One and a Half Times the Price

A second hive costs $470, not $900. The woodenware is $285 and the bees $185, and the suit, smoker, tool, and feeder already live in your shed. Two colonies run about $1,400 with an extractor, $1,200 without.

Buy the second one. Roughly three colonies in ten die each winter, and a single-hive keeper who loses that coin flip sits out the season. Two hives also let you move a frame of eggs from the strong colony into a queenless one.

Package or Nucleus: What the Extra $70 Buys

A package is three pounds of bees shaken into a box with a caged queen. A nucleus is five frames of drawn comb, brood in every stage, and a laying queen the bees already accept. The nucleus costs $60 to $90 more and is worth it in most of the country.

Two things make up the difference. First, comb is expensive to build: bees burn about 6 pounds of honey for every pound of wax, and twenty deep frames from foundation represent 12 to 18 pounds of honey that never reaches your shelves. Second, and larger, is calendar time. A package needs three to four weeks to draw a box, and the first brood that queen lays takes 21 days to become foragers. Call it six weeks before the colony forages at full strength. A nucleus is foraging in days.

In Zone 5 or 6, six weeks from an April install lands in mid-June, right as clover finishes. A month of strong flow is worth 20 to 30 pounds of surplus, more than the $70 premium. The Deep South is the exception: its flow starts in February, so a March package still catches most of it.

What You Can Skip, and What You Cannot

Skip the extractor in year one

Most local clubs lend or rent one for $0 to $25 a day, and there are more than a thousand clubs in the United States. Crush-and-strain also works for a first harvest: cut the comb out, mash it, let it drain through a strainer into a bottling bucket. You lose the comb and the bees rebuild it, which costs them a few pounds of honey, and you save $170.

Skip the Flow Hive until you have run a wooden one

A Flow super costs more than a complete wooden hive with bees in it. It works and skips the extracting step, but the money buys no protection, no bees, and no second colony. A $75 jacket and jeans cover an inspection as well as a $175 suit. Buy woodenware first.

Do not skip the second brood box

A single deep forces a swarm in June, and a swarm takes half your foragers three weeks before the flow. The $35 box and $30 of frames are the cheapest insurance in the budget.

The Yearly Bill After Year One

Ongoing costs are small and mostly sugar. A colony needs 60 pounds of sugar for fall syrup in a temperate climate, 80 where winters are harsh, and 40 where they are mild. Mixed 2:1, 60 pounds of sugar plus 30 pounds of water makes 11 gallons, more than one hive takes unless the fall flow fails.

Recurring cost Per hive per year Note
Sugar, 60 lb $45–$66 Only what the hive does not store itself
Varroa treatment $45 One August round plus a spring check
Jars and lids $50 40 pints on a 40-lb harvest
Replacement queen, every other year $14 $28 a queen, split across two seasons
Club dues, state apiary registration $25–$50 Registration is required in some states
Replacement package after a winter loss $150–$200 every third year Roughly 30 percent annual loss

Budget $160 to $210 a hive, and keep a line for replacement bees. The keeper who plans for a dead colony every few years spends less than the one who treats a loss as a surprise.

Home honey is not cheaper than store honey until the fourth season, and only at farmers market prices. At $3 a pound wholesale the same hive never breaks even.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to start beekeeping with one hive?

Between $600 and $1,100 for one hive, bees, gear, and tools, with about $900 the realistic middle. The spread comes from assembled versus unassembled woodenware, package versus nucleus, and whether you buy an extractor.

Can you make money beekeeping?

Not in the first three years. One hive returns 30 to 60 pounds a year once established, worth $240 to $720 at $8 to $12 a pound retail, against about $160 a year in sugar, treatment, and jars. Cost per pound falls under $12 in year four.

How much does it cost to keep bees after the first year?

About $160 to $210 per hive: 60 pounds of sugar for fall syrup, $45 for varroa treatment, $50 for jars, plus club dues. Add $150 to $200 when a colony dies over winter, which happens to roughly three in ten.

Do I need to buy a honey extractor to start?

No. Most local clubs lend or rent one for $0 to $25 a day, and crush-and-strain works on a first harvest for the cost of a bucket and strainer. Buy the $170 unit after you have three hives producing at once.

The Budget to Write Down

Plan $900 for one hive and $1,200 for two, spend the extra $70 on a nucleus, borrow an extractor for two seasons, and treat for mites in August whether or not you see any. That path gives 40 pounds in year two and 55 in year three. Check the local ordinance and state apiary registration before the bees arrive.

The exception is the long-season South. A nucleus installed in March in Zone 8 or 9 can put up 20 to 30 pounds of surplus in the first year, and that single harvest moves break-even up by a full season.
