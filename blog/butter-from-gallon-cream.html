How Much Butter From a Gallon of Cream?

Published: August 6, 2026 · 8 min read

A US gallon of heavy cream at 36% butterfat makes about 3 to 3.5 pounds of butter — roughly 12 to 14 quarter-pound sticks — plus 2 to 2.5 quarts of buttermilk. If you are churning thinner gravity-skimmed cream at 20–25% fat, expect closer to 2 to 2.4 pounds from the same gallon.

The number swings by almost double across those two scenarios, because you are not really churning cream — you are churning the fat suspended in it. Below is the full yield table, the milk-to-cream math for four dairy animals, and the mistakes that quietly cost you half a pound a batch.

Butter Yield by Cream Fat Percentage

One US gallon of cream weighs approximately 8.4 pounds. Multiply that by the fat percentage to get the butterfat available, subtract the 5–12% of fat that stays behind in the buttermilk, then add back the water that finished butter legally retains. The result:

Cream type Butterfat Fat in 1 gallon Finished butter Sticks Buttermilk
Gravity-skimmed top cream 20% 1.7 lb ~1.9 lb 7–8 ~3 qt
Light cream / half-and-half blend 25% 2.1 lb ~2.4 lb 9–10 ~2.9 qt
Whipping cream 30% 2.5 lb ~2.9 lb 11–12 ~2.7 qt
Heavy cream 35% 2.9 lb ~3.3 lb 13 ~2.4 qt
Heavy whipping cream (typical US retail) 36% 3.0 lb ~3.4 lb 13–14 ~2.3 qt
Double / manufacturing cream 40% 3.4 lb ~3.8 lb 15 ~2.2 qt

Those figures assume about 92% fat recovery, which is a realistic average for a home churn. Scale down freely: a quart of heavy cream is a quarter of a gallon, so it gives roughly 13 to 14 ounces of butter — just under a pound, or about three and a half sticks.

Why Finished Butter Weighs More Than the Fat You Started With

This is the single most confusing part of butter math, and it is why two honest sources will quote you different numbers for the same gallon.

Butter is not pure fat. Under the US federal standard of identity it must contain at least 80% milkfat, with the remaining 16–18% being water and about 1–2% milk solids. So once you know how much fat you recovered, you divide by roughly 0.81 — not multiply by one — to get the weight that shows up on your kitchen scale.

Worked example for a gallon of 36% cream:

  • 8.4 lb of cream × 36% = 3.02 lb of butterfat in the jar
  • × 92% recovery = 2.78 lb of fat actually captured in the butter grains
  • ÷ 0.81 (butter is 81% fat) = 3.43 lb of finished butter

If your scale reads about 20% higher than the calculator's estimate, nothing has gone wrong; you simply have well-hydrated butter rather than over-worked butter.

How Much Milk Does a Gallon of Cream Take?

If you are buying cream, skip this section. If you are milking your own animal, the gallon of cream is the hard part — the churning is trivial by comparison.

The milk requirement depends entirely on the animal's butterfat. The table below assumes a mechanical separator recovering about 95% of available fat, and a home churn recovering 92% of that:

Animal / breed Typical butterfat Butter per gallon of milk Milk per pound of butter Milk for 1 gal of 35% cream
Holstein cow 3.5% ~5.2 oz ~3.1 gal ~10.3 gal
Jersey / Guernsey cow 4.8% ~7.1 oz ~2.2 gal ~7.5 gal
Dairy goat 3.8% ~5.6 oz ~2.8 gal ~9.5 gal
Dairy sheep (East Friesian) 7.0% ~10.4 oz ~1.5 gal ~5.1 gal

The widely quoted industry benchmark is about 21 pounds of milk — roughly 2.5 gallons — per pound of butter. That figure comes from commercial creameries running continuous separators and churns at near-total fat recovery on 3.9% milk. At home, plan on 20–25% more milk than the industry number for the same pound.

Gravity skimming vs. a cream separator

Let a gallon of raw cow milk sit in a wide, shallow container for 12–24 hours and the cream rises into a distinct layer you can ladle or siphon off. It is free, it needs no equipment, and it typically recovers only 60–75% of the butterfat, at a thickness somewhere between 18% and 30% depending on how greedy you were at the cream line.

A hand-crank or electric separator pulls 95%+ of the fat and lets you dial the cream to 35–45%. On a Jersey cow producing 4 gallons a day, that is roughly a pound of extra butter every week — the machine pays for itself inside a season. Goat milk is the special case: it is naturally homogenized, with fat globules so small that gravity separation is slow and incomplete, so a separator is effectively mandatory. Goat owners more often route milk toward cheese instead, where the yield math is kinder — our Goat Cheese Yield Calculator covers chèvre, feta, and hard-cheese conversions.

Churning Method Changes the Yield Too

Different churns break the fat globule membrane with different efficiency, and the ones that finish fastest are not always the ones that extract the most fat. All figures below are for one gallon of 36% cream:

Method Typical time Fat recovery Butter yield
Hand churn or shaken jar 20–45 min ~90% 3 lb 6 oz
Stand mixer, paddle attachment 8–20 min ~92% 3 lb 7 oz
Electric butter churn 5–15 min ~95% 3 lb 9 oz
Food processor 2–8 min ~88% 3 lb 5 oz

The food processor is the speed champion and the yield laggard: it warms the cream several degrees and throws fine fat droplets into the buttermilk before the grains can gather. Fine for a quart — but on a full gallon the four ounces you lose are worth ten extra minutes on a slower machine. Whatever you use, never fill it past half capacity; cream needs headspace to tumble.

Four Things That Quietly Cost You Butter

1. Churning cold cream

The single biggest yield killer. Fat globules need to be soft enough to fuse but firm enough to hold shape, which happens in a narrow window around 58–65°F (14–18°C). Straight-from-the-fridge cream at 38°F can churn for an hour and still leave visible fat in the buttermilk. Cream above 70°F flips the other way: it goes to a greasy, soft mass that will not gather into grains and holds far too much buttermilk. Pull the cream out and let it sit on the counter for an hour before you start.

2. Using cream the day you separate it

Fresh cream churns, but ripened cream churns better. Holding cream at 40°F for 24–48 hours lets the fat crystallize and the globules aggregate, and it consistently shortens churn time while raising recovery a few percent. If you want cultured butter, add a mesophilic culture or a few tablespoons of yogurt or true buttermilk, hold at 70°F for 12 hours until it smells faintly tangy, then chill back to 60°F and churn. The diacetyl produced during ripening is what gives European-style butter its flavor — the same bacteria used for soft cheese, as covered in our complete cheese making guide.

3. Skipping the wash

Once the grains gather, drain the buttermilk and rinse the butter in cold water until it runs clear — usually three or four changes. This is a keeping-quality step, not a yield step: residual buttermilk sours within days and turns good butter rancid at the edges. Washed butter keeps two to three weeks in the fridge instead of four or five days.

4. Over-working the finished butter

Here is where washing turns into a yield problem. Pressing out every last drop drives the water content below the 16% that butter is supposed to hold, and on a gallon batch that can mean four to six ounces of lost weight and a crumbly, waxy texture. Work it just until no more cloudy liquid appears, then stop. Salt at about 1% of butter weight — roughly half an ounce, or 2½ teaspoons of fine salt, for a 3.4-pound batch — if you want lightly salted butter.

What to Do With Two Quarts of Buttermilk

Real churned buttermilk is not the thick, tangy cultured product sold in cartons. It is thin, faintly sweet, and high in the milk proteins and lecithin that washed out of the fat globule membranes — which is exactly why it makes such tender baked goods. It keeps about a week refrigerated and freezes well in 1-cup portions.

The obvious destination is baking. Pancakes, biscuits, cornbread, soda bread, and buttermilk-brined chicken each take a cup or two, and the acidity reacts with baking soda for extra lift. If you bake in any volume, our Bread Baking Calculator scales hydration and flour weights when you swap buttermilk in for part of the water. The rest can go into ricotta — heating buttermilk with a splash of whole milk and a little acid recovers the last of the protein as a soft fresh cheese, and the Cheese Making Calculator gives milk-to-yield ratios for that and a dozen other styles.

Storing Homemade Butter

Well-washed, lightly salted butter holds two to three weeks in the refrigerator and six to twelve months in the freezer when wrapped tightly in parchment and sealed in an airtight bag. Unsalted or unwashed butter is closer to one week refrigerated.

What you must not do is can it. Butter, cream, milk, and cheese have no tested home-canning process — fat conducts heat poorly and unpredictably, so no one can guarantee the cold spot in the jar ever reached a safe temperature. The same logic rules out several other tempting homestead staples, which we cover in the guide to water bath vs pressure canning. Freeze the surplus instead; frozen butter thaws with no loss of texture, which is more than can be said for most preserved dairy.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much butter does a gallon of heavy cream make?

About 3 to 3.5 pounds — roughly 12 to 14 quarter-pound sticks — from a gallon of store-bought heavy whipping cream at 36% butterfat, plus 2 to 2.5 quarts of buttermilk. Thinner gravity-skimmed cream at 20% fat yields closer to 1.9 pounds from the same gallon.

How many gallons of milk does it take to make a pound of butter?

Commercial creameries use about 21 pounds of milk, or 2.5 gallons, per pound. At home expect roughly 3.1 gallons of Holstein milk at 3.5% fat, 2.2 gallons of Jersey milk at 4.8%, or just 1.5 gallons of sheep milk at 7%. Hand skimming instead of a separator pushes all of those numbers up 25–40%.

Why is my butter yield lower than expected?

Almost always cream temperature, cream age, or over-working. Churn at 58–65°F, ripen the cream 24–48 hours in the fridge first, and stop pressing once the liquid runs clear. Ultra-pasteurized cream and food-processor churning each cost another 2–4%.

Can I make butter from store-bought ultra-pasteurized cream?

Yes, though it churns slower and tastes flatter. The 280°F flash heat denatures the proteins around the fat globules, so they resist clumping. Add five to ten minutes to your churn time and expect a slightly lower yield than regular pasteurized or raw cream of the same fat percentage.

How much buttermilk do you get from a gallon of cream?

About 2 to 2.5 quarts (8–10 cups) from 35–36% cream, and close to 3 quarts from 20% cream. It is thin and sweet rather than thick and cultured, but it performs beautifully in pancakes, biscuits, quick breads, and brines.
