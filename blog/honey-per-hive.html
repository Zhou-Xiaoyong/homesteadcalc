How Much Honey Does One Hive Produce Per Year?

Published: August 17, 2026 · 9 min read

One healthy, established beehive produces about 30 to 60 pounds of surplus honey per year—roughly 2.5 to 5 gallons. That is the honey you get to keep after the bees have set aside enough to survive winter. A stacked-box Langstroth hive in a good location can push past 100 lbs, while a first-year hive often gives you little or nothing at all. The national average across U.S. beekeepers lands near 55 lbs per colony in a typical season.

The honest answer, though, is that "how much honey does one hive produce" has a huge range—anywhere from zero to well over 100 pounds. Your yield depends mostly on where you live, what kind of hive you keep, how healthy the colony is, and plain weather luck. Below is the realistic breakdown, what the bees keep for themselves, and how that harvest translates into jars and dollars. The Short Answer: Surplus Honey by Hive Type

"Surplus" is the key word. It is the honey left over after the colony has stored enough to feed itself through winter. The table below shows what a single, well-managed hive typically yields in a good year:

Hive Type Typical Surplus (lbs/yr) Best-Case Surplus Harvest Style
Langstroth (10-frame) 40–100 lbs 120+ lbs Extract (spinnable frames)
Langstroth (8-frame) 30–80 lbs 100 lbs Extract (spinnable frames)
Flow Hive 30–70 lbs 90 lbs Tap and drain
Warre 20–50 lbs 60 lbs Crush and strain
Top Bar 15–40 lbs 50 lbs Crush and strain
Nucleus (starter) 0–10 lbs 15 lbs Rarely harvested

For most backyard beekeepers running one or two Langstroth hives, 30–60 lbs per hive is the number to expect. That single figure assumes a strong colony, a decent nectar flow, and a keeper who leaves enough honey on the hive for winter.

Rule of thumb: Plan on roughly 1 pound of honey per week of good foraging weather per strong hive. A season with 35–50 solid nectar weeks delivers that 40–60 lb surplus; a wet, cool year can cut it in half.

Surplus vs. Total: What the Bees Keep

It is easy to confuse "what the hive makes" with "what you get to harvest," but they are very different. A colony does not produce honey for you—it produces honey to feed itself. Through a full year, a single hive can pack away 100–150 lbs of honey total, but most of that is the colony's own pantry.

A hive in a cold-winter climate needs 60–100 lbs of stored honey just to survive until spring. In a mild southern climate, that winter reserve might drop to 40–60 lbs. Extension services consistently cite these figures because they are the margin between a hive that makes it through March and one that starves in February. As the beekeeper, you harvest only what remains above that safety line.

This is also why you should never harvest a first-year hive's honey. A new colony needs every drop it stores to build up and overwinter. Taking it almost guarantees a dead hive by spring.

What That Harvest Looks Like in Jars

Numbers in pounds are useful, but what you really end up with is jars on a shelf. Honey weighs about 12 ounces per standard 1-pint (16 fl oz) jar when filled—close enough that 1 pound of honey ≈ 1 pint jar. Here is how a single hive's surplus translates:

Surplus per Hive 1-Pint Jars (16 oz) Quart Jars (32 oz) 12 oz Honey Bears
20 lbs (weak/small hive) ~20 jars ~10 jars ~27 bears
40 lbs (average Langstroth) ~40 jars ~20 jars ~53 bears
60 lbs (good year) ~60 jars ~30 jars ~80 bears
100 lbs (strong hive, great year) ~100 jars ~50 jars ~133 bears

For a family of four, a single 40–60 lb harvest is plenty for a year of toast, tea, baking, and gifts—the average American eats only about 1.3 lbs of honey annually. Two hives give you a comfortable surplus to share.

Why Your First Year Is Different

If you are starting beekeeping this spring and dreaming of shelves of honey, temper those expectations. A first-year hive almost always produces far less than the tables above suggest—typically 20–40% of a mature hive's surplus, and often zero.

There are three reasons. First, a new package or nuc spends its first months building comb from scratch; comb-building consumes enormous amounts of sugar and nectar that would otherwise become surplus. Second, the colony is small for most of the season and only reaches full forager strength late in summer—often after the main honey flow has peaked. Third, a wise first-year keeper leaves all the honey on the hive so the bees enter winter strong. Harvesting year one is how beginners lose colonies.

First-year reality: Treat year one as "keep the bees alive." If you get a few jars, call it a bonus. Real harvests start in year two, once the comb is drawn and the colony is established.

What Pushes a Single Hive Above or Below Average

Two beekeepers a few miles apart can get wildly different yields from identical hives. The biggest levers:

Location and Forage

This is the single largest factor. A hive surrounded by clover, wildflowers, orchards, and weeds—common in suburban and rural-suburban areas—will outproduce a hive in a monoculture corn-and-soybean landscape. Regions with long growing seasons (Southern U.S., parts of California) routinely beat short-season northern states. The length of your local nectar flow matters more than almost anything else.

Colony Health (Especially Varroa)

Untreated varroa mite infestations can cut a hive's honey yield by half or more, and they are the leading cause of winter colony loss. A solid integrated pest management routine—monitoring mite levels and treating on schedule—is the difference between a 60 lb harvest and a dead hive.

Weather and the Honey Flow

Bees cannot forage in cold, rain, or drought. A two-week stretch of bad weather during the main flow can erase a third of your season. The most experienced beekeepers plan around averages and accept that some years simply underperform.

Management Timing

Adding honey supers before the colony is cramped, preventing swarms (which split your forager force in half right before the flow), and requeening aging queens every year or two all push a single hive toward the top of its range. These are learnable skills that pay off within two or three seasons.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much honey does one beehive produce in pounds?

A healthy, established Langstroth hive yields about 30–60 lbs of surplus honey per year in a good season, with a national average near 55 lbs per colony. Top-bar and Warre hives produce less (15–50 lbs), and a first-year hive often yields little or nothing.

How many jars of honey come from one hive?

One pound of honey fills roughly one 1-pint (16 oz) jar. So a 30–60 lb harvest equals about 30–60 pint jars, or roughly 15–30 quart jars. At 12 oz per standard honey bear, that is 40–80 bears per hive.

How much honey does a first-year hive produce?

Usually very little. A new colony spends its first season building comb, raising bees, and storing winter food, so it often produces only 20–40% of a mature hive's surplus—and many beginners harvest nothing at all in year one. Year two is when yields climb.

Does hive type change how much honey one hive makes?

Yes. Stackable Langstroth hives let you add honey supers and are the most productive (30–100+ lbs surplus). Horizontal top-bar hives (15–40 lbs) and Warre hives (20–50 lbs) yield less because you cannot expand vertically and must crush natural comb to extract.

How much is one hive's honey worth?

Retail honey sells for roughly $8–12 per pound at most U.S. farmers markets. A 30–60 lb surplus is therefore worth about $240–720 per hive before jars and labels. Most keepers value the honey for home use and gifting rather than as a profit center.

Putting It Together

So, how much honey does one hive produce? Plan on 30–60 lbs of surplus from a healthy Langstroth in a normal year, expect less from natural-comb hives, and expect almost nothing from your very first hive. Leave the bees their winter stores, manage varroa, and time your supers with the local nectar flow, and a single hive can reliably put a year's worth of honey on your table.
