How Much Salt to Brine a Turkey?

Published: November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

A 20-pound turkey takes 3 cups of Diamond Crystal kosher salt and 3 1/4 gallons of water. That is 430 grams of salt, 2 percent of the bird and the brine weighed together, and a full cup less than most recipes tell you to use.

The difference is the bucket. Brine recipes are written for a stockpot that holds the bird and four gallons at once. A five-gallon pail does not, so the salt has to follow the water that actually fits around the bird.

Two Percent, Measured Against Bird Plus Water

Brine strength is a percentage of everything in the pail, not a spoon count. Two percent is a mild brine, and it roasts like a turkey rather than a ham. Three percent starts tasting cured at the bone.

Twenty pounds is 9,072 grams. Three and a quarter gallons of water is 12,300 grams. Together that is 21.4 kilograms, and 2 percent of it is 428 grams, which is three cups of Diamond Crystal.

Water to cover works out to about a gallon per six pounds of bird. Below that the breast floats above the line and brines unevenly.

A Five-Gallon Pail Does Not Hold Four Gallons

Five gallons is 1,155 cubic inches. A 20-pound turkey takes up roughly 1 3/4 gallons of that, leaving 3 1/4 gallons of liquid and an inch of headspace. Recipes calling for four gallons are assuming a 22-quart stockpot or a cooler.

Work backward instead of guessing. Set the bird in the pail, fill until it covers by an inch, lift the bird out, and measure what is left. That is your water volume, and the salt follows.

A zip bag uses less brine and needs flipping every few hours. Recalculate the percentage on the water you actually poured, not on the gallons above.

The pail also has to sit somewhere cold. Five-gallon buckets stand 14 1/2 inches tall and most refrigerator shelves clear 10 to 12. If it does not fit, the plan is a cooler, not the fridge.

Which Salt, and What a Cup Actually Weighs

Kosher salt is flaky, and flake size is the entire argument. One cup of Diamond Crystal weighs 140 grams. One cup of Morton kosher weighs 200. One cup of table salt weighs 290, twice the salt in the same volume.

Two hosts follow the same one-cup-per-gallon line and end up 30 percent apart, then blame the bird. Weigh the salt once and the argument is over.

No scale, no problem. A tablespoon of Diamond Crystal is about 9 grams, so 430 grams is 48 tablespoons, exactly three cups. Count level tablespoons and skip the scoop.

Wet Brine, Dry Brine, or Buy It Salted

Five routes put salt into a turkey, and they differ by a factor of twenty in price.

Dry brine is half a teaspoon of Diamond Crystal per pound, which is 10 teaspoons for a 20-pound bird, a shade over three tablespoons. Salt it Monday, set it on a rack uncovered, and the surface dries while the salt works inward. That dry surface is what browns.

Kosher birds are salted during processing and rinsed before packing. A second soak stacks another 2 percent on top, and nothing at the table takes it back out.

Basted birds carry an injected solution, up to 3 percent of weight and printed on the label. That is a brine already, done at the plant and paid for at the per-pound price.

Twenty Hours, Held Under 40 Degrees

One hour per pound is the working rate, so a 20-pound bird sits for 20 hours inside a 12-to-24-hour window. Under 12 and the center stays bland. Past 24 the meat turns springy and the salt keeps climbing.

Cold is the step people skip. Bird and brine stay at 40 degrees or below for the whole soak, which means the refrigerator or a cooler with ice, never a garage shelf in November.

Cooler arithmetic: 2 gallons of water plus 1 1/2 gallons of ice, about 12 pounds, lands the bath near 38 degrees. Check at hour eight and add a frozen water bottle if the ice is gone.

Flip the bird at the halfway mark if it is bagged. One side always sits against the plastic, and that side brines slower than the side facing open water.

What the Brine Changes on the Table

Gravy. Drippings from a brined bird are already seasoned, and the first hour of roasting weeps salt water into the pan. Leave the salt out of the base and taste at the end. The gravy arithmetic for twelve is written for exactly this bird.

Stuffing. Do not salt it. A 15-cup batch of cubed bread draws salt out of the cavity walls while it sits, and the pan drippings finish the job.

Skin. A wet-brined bird comes out pale and waterlogged. Pat it dry, then leave it uncovered on a rack in the fridge for 12 to 24 hours before roasting. Skip the dry and the skin steams instead of browning, whatever the oven does.

Its Himalayan salt row runs 3 percent of meat weight, 272 grams on a 20-pounder, which sounds stronger than the 2 percent above. It is, because the brine here spreads its salt through 3 1/4 gallons of water before the bird touches it. Two jobs, two numbers.

One more figure worth keeping: a wet-brined bird gains 4 to 8 percent of its weight in water. Your 20-pound bird weighs 20.8 to 21.6 when it comes out, which matters if the roasting pan or the oven was already tight. The freezer and shelf arithmetic covers the space question for the days before.

If the Bird Is Yours

A home-raised bird runs leaner than a broad-breasted commodity turkey, and heritage strains leaner still. Lean meat is where brining earns its keep, and the 2 percent figure does not change.

Two cautions. Drain the cavity first, because rinse water left inside dilutes the brine. And thaw completely: a 20-pounder needs four to five days in the refrigerator, and a half-frozen bird oversalts outside while the center sits at 30 degrees.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much salt do I need to brine a 20-pound turkey?

Three cups of Diamond Crystal kosher salt, 430 grams, in 3 1/4 gallons of water. That is 2 percent of the bird and the brine together. With Morton kosher use 2 1/8 cups; with table salt, 1 1/2 cups.

Can I brine a kosher or self-basting turkey?

No. Kosher birds are salted in processing and basted birds carry up to 3 percent injected solution, so either one is already brined. A second soak doubles the salt, and nothing you do at the table takes it back out.

How long should a turkey stay in the brine?

One hour per pound, so 20 hours for a 20-pound bird, inside a 12-to-24-hour window. Keep it at 40 degrees or below the whole time. Past 24 hours the meat reads like cured pork instead of roast turkey.

Do I have to rinse the bird after brining?

Rinse it, pat it dry, then air-dry it uncovered on a rack for 12 to 24 hours before roasting. The rinse clears surface salt; the air-dry is what gives you crisp skin. Skip the dry and the skin steams.

The Numbers to Write Down

Three cups of Diamond Crystal, 3 1/4 gallons of water, 20 hours at 38 degrees, then 12 to 24 hours uncovered in the fridge. Salt costs about $4 against $10 to $20 for a pre-brined bird, and the pail is a one-time $7.

The exception is a kosher or self-basting bird in your freezer. Brine either one and nothing at the table corrects it.

The rest of the menu arithmetic sits in the same series: pounds of potatoes, rolls by the dozen, pumpkins per pie, and butter by the tablespoon. Season the bird, then leave the gravy base alone.
