How Long to Dehydrate Apples (Time and Temperature)

Published: September 17, 2026 · 8 min read

Dehydrate apples at 135°F (57°C) for 6 to 12 hours. Quarter-inch slices — the standard for snacking and storage — are usually dry in 7 to 9 hours; thin eighth-inch slices finish in 5 to 6; thick rings and wedges can run 12 hours or longer. That range is not a manufacturer's cop-out. It reflects the three variables you actually control: how thick you cut the fruit, how much water the variety holds, and how full your trays are stacked.

This guide pins the time down with a thickness table, the doneness tests that end the guesswork, and equivalent schedules for an oven, an air fryer, and the sun. The Dehydrator Time & Temperature Guide covers 40-plus foods in one table if you want the numbers without the reading.

Quick Answer: Apple Drying Time by Slice Thickness

Temperature stays fixed; thickness is what moves the clock. Slice everything on the same setting, and the tray finishes together:

Cut Thickness Temp Drying Time Best For
Thin slices 1/8 inch 135°F 5–6 hours Crisp chips, fruit powder, granola
Standard slices 1/4 inch 135°F 7–9 hours Snacking, pies, oatmeal, trail mix
Thick slices 3/8 inch 135°F 10–12 hours Hearty snacking, rehydrating for baking
Rings (cored, unpeeled) 1/4–1/2 inch 135°F 10–14 hours Wreaths, crafts, long-term storage
Wedges / quarters 1/2–3/4 inch 135°F 12–16 hours Rehydrating for sauce and pies
Fruit leather (puree) 1/8 inch spread 135°F 6–10 hours Roll-ups, lunchbox snacks

Two rules keep the range from drifting further: slice uniform, and do not overload. A tray of mixed 1/8- and 3/8-inch slices scorches the thin ones while the thick ones stay damp. Aim for 1.5 to 2 pounds of raw sliced apple per tray, spaced so no two pieces touch.

Why the Same Recipe Takes 6 Hours One Day and 12 the Next

Apples are roughly 84 to 86 percent water. All that time is water leaving, and several things change how fast it can leave:

  • Variety and ripeness. Dense, firm apples dry predictably. Overripe or mealy fruit has already broken down internally and collapses into sticky sheets.
  • Tray loading. Six crowded trays take noticeably longer than three sparse ones — the fan has to move the same air through more obstruction.
  • Humidity. A humid kitchen in August dries slower than the same load in a dry January kitchen.
  • Pretreatment. An ascorbic acid or lemon dip adds surface water. Pat the slices dry first and the difference disappears.

Firm, tart varieties dry best: Granny Smith, Braeburn, Pink Lady, and Honeycrisp all hold shape. Soft early-season apples tend to produce leathery clumps rather than clean chips.

The 135°F Question: Why Not Hotter or Cooler

National Center for Home Food Preservation guidance puts fruit drying at 135°F. Below about 125°F, drying slows enough to give mold and yeast time to work, and fruit in a poorly ventilated dehydrator can start to ferment. Above 145°F the slice case-hardens: the surface seals while the interior is still wet, so you get a piece that feels done and grows mold in the jar three weeks later.

Some older dehydrator manuals recommend starting at 145°F for the first hour and then dropping to 135°F. It works, but on a machine with an accurate thermostat it buys nothing; one temperature start to finish gives a more even result.

Step by Step: Prep That Sets the Clock

1. Wash, core, and slice uniformly

Cut on a mandoline or slicer set to 1/4 inch for the standard schedule. Peeling is optional — the peel adds fiber and helps the slice hold together. Three medium apples are about a pound, or roughly half a tray at 1/4 inch.

2. Pretreat to stop browning

Apples oxidize fast once cut. Dissolve one teaspoon of ascorbic acid (or a commercial Fruit Fresh blend) in a quart of cold water and soak the slices three to five minutes; equal parts bottled lemon juice and water is the household substitute. A sulfite dip holds color and vitamin C best but can trigger reactions in sulfite-sensitive people. Pat dry after dipping.

3. Load the trays without crowding

Arrange in a single layer with a half inch between pieces and an inch of clearance at the tray edge. Pieces that touch dry into one lump that stays damp in the middle.

4. Dry, rotate, and test

Run at 135°F, rotating trays top to bottom and front to back every two hours for the first half of the cycle. Dehydrators have hot spots, and rotating is the difference between an even batch and one with a few scorched slices. Start testing at six hours, then every 30 to 60 minutes.

How to Tell Dried Apples Are Actually Done

Drying by the clock alone is how people end up with moldy jars. Judge by feel instead:

  • Leathery and pliable, not brittle. A properly dried slice bends without cracking. If it snaps, you overshot — good for chips, poor for rehydrating.
  • No moisture at the cut edge. Squeeze a slice hard; nothing should bead on the surface.
  • No shiny damp interior. Tear one open — the inside should look dry and uniform, not glossy.
  • Cool before judging. Warm dried fruit feels softer and moister than it will at room temperature. Let a test slice cool five minutes.

Home-dried apples are not bone-dry like commercial ones: the target is "leathery," roughly 20 percent residual moisture, which is why conditioning is not optional.

Oven, Air Fryer, and Sun: Equivalent Schedules

No dehydrator, or the trays are already full? The times shift but the temperature target does not:

Method Set To Time for 1/4-Inch Slices Watch Out For
Dehydrator (stack trays) 135°F 7–9 hours Hot spots — rotate trays every 2 hours
Convection oven 135–140°F 8–10 hours Many ovens bottom out at 170°F; prop the door open and verify with a thermometer
Standard oven, door propped 140°F (door ajar) 8–12 hours Uneven heat, higher energy cost, no airflow
Air fryer (dehydrate mode) 135°F 6–10 hours Small capacity — expect 1–2 apples per batch
Sun drying 95°F+ and low humidity 2–4 days Only reliable in hot, dry, windy climates; USDA does not recommend it in humid regions

An oven needs a real thermometer, not the dial. Many residential ovens cannot hold 135°F at all; the ones that can often cycle 25 degrees around the set point. If your oven cannot stay under 145°F, use the air fryer or borrow a dehydrator.

Yield: How Many Apples Make How Much

Apples lose 80 to 85 percent of their weight on the tray, so plan around a 5:1 to 7:1 fresh-to-dried ratio:

Fresh Apples Weight Dried Yield Approx. Cups Dried
3 medium apples 1 lb 2.5–3 oz Scant 1 cup
12 medium apples 4 lb 10–12 oz 3–3.5 cups
30 medium apples 10 lb 1.5–2 lb 8–9 cups

That math cuts both ways. A single tree can bury a household in fruit: a mature semi-dwarf yields 75 to 125 pounds in a season, which at 6:1 is 12 to 20 pounds of dried slices from one tree. Conditioning and Storage

Cool the dried slices completely — at least 30 minutes, uncovered, on the trays. Then condition them: pack loosely in a glass jar, seal it, and shake it once a day for seven to ten days. Any slice that was under-dried releases its remaining moisture into the jar, showing up as condensation on the glass or clumping. If you see either, return the batch to the dehydrator for another hour or two and start conditioning over.

After conditioning, store in an airtight container in a cool, dark place — 6 to 12 months is a realistic shelf life, longer in vacuum-sealed bags or the freezer. The Vegetable Storage Chart printable covers storage conditions for the rest of the harvest.

Using Dried Apples

Rehydrate by soaking in hot water or apple juice for 30 to 60 minutes, roughly one cup of liquid per cup of dried slices. Simmer the soaking liquid down and you have a syrup for pies and crisps without added sugar. Dried slices also grind into a powder that thickens sauces and flavors oatmeal. Per USDA food data, a cup of dried apple slices carries around 210 calories and 6 grams of fiber — about four times the energy of the same volume of fresh apple, worth remembering when a recipe calls for "a handful."

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to dehydrate apples at 135°F?

Plan on 6 to 12 hours at 135°F. Quarter-inch slices are usually finished in 7 to 9 hours, eighth-inch slices in 5 to 6, and thick wedges or rings can run 12 hours or more. Test at the six-hour mark and then every 30 to 60 minutes.

Do you have to peel apples before dehydrating?

No. Peels add fiber, hold the slice together, and dry fine as long as the slices are uniform. Peel only if you want a softer texture for snacking or if you are dehydrating mealy or blemished fruit. Wash, core, and slice either way.

Should apples be soaked in lemon juice before drying?

Yes, if you care about color. A three to five minute dip in one teaspoon of ascorbic acid per quart of water, or an equal-parts lemon juice and water dip, keeps slices from browning. It does not change drying time, only appearance and vitamin C retention.

How do you know when dried apples are done?

They should feel leathery and pliable, not brittle. Squeeze a slice: no moisture should bead at the cut edge, and it should spring back rather than snap or stay crushed. When torn open, the inside should show no shiny damp areas.

How many fresh apples does it take to make a pound of dried apples?

Roughly 5 to 7 pounds of fresh apples yield one pound of dried slices, because apples are about 85 percent water. Three medium apples weigh about a pound, so a pound of dried slices starts as 15 to 21 apples.
